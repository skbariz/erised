================================================================================
GENERAL IMPLEMENTATION WARNINGS
================================================================================

1. These papers often depend on data that is harder than daily prices:
   - analyst recommendations and revisions
   - insider transactions
   - short-sale activity or short interest
   - options volume, implied vols, and option surface data
   - Google search volume or alternative attention data
   - repurchase announcements, share issuance, IPO/SEO events

2. Do not use "event date" when "public availability date" is needed.
   For example, an insider trade date, filing date, and market availability date can differ.

3. Use conservative execution delays:
   - daily price signals: next close or next open
   - analyst/insider/filing signals: after public release plus one trading day unless timestamps are precise
   - options/intraday signals: use only if timestamped and your backtester supports intraday execution

4. Many Set 2 signals are capacity-constrained.
   Short-term reversal, options-volume signals, and attention signals can decay quickly after costs.

5. Long/short implementations require borrow assumptions.
   For short-seller, issuance, high-sentiment, high-attention, and high-overpricing signals,
   always run a long-only "avoid/underweight" version alongside the short version.

6. For S&P 500 specifically, many anomalies may be weaker than in all-stock universes.
   Large-cap stocks are more liquid, more covered, and more arbitraged.

THE 20 ADDITIONAL PAPERS AND IMPLEMENTATION LEARNINGS

01. Moskowitz and Grinblatt (1999)
Title: Do Industries Explain Momentum?
Source: https://ideas.repec.org/a/bla/jfinan/v54y1999i4p1249-1290.html
Core idea:
Industry-level momentum explains much of individual-stock momentum. Past winning industries
continue to outperform past losing industries, and industry momentum remains profitable even
after controlling for size, value, individual momentum, and microstructure effects.
Implementation translation:
- Feature: industry_return_6m = value-weighted trailing industry return over prior 6 months.
- Assign each S&P 500 stock to GICS sector/industry.
- Rank industries by past return, then allocate to stocks inside top industries.
Useful S&P 500 algorithms:
- Long top 3-5 industries by 6-month return, equal-weight stocks inside each industry.
- Industry momentum overlay: multiply stock momentum score by industry momentum score.
- Long top industry stocks, short bottom industry stocks, sector exposure controlled by design.
Main risks:
- Concentration in one macro theme.
- Industry classifications change over time.
- Can underperform during sharp sector rotations.

02. Blitz, Huij, and Martens (2011)
Title: Residual Momentum
Source: https://repub.eur.nl/pub/22252
Core idea:
Conventional momentum has time-varying exposures to common risk factors. Ranking stocks on
residual returns after removing market/size/value exposures can produce cleaner, more stable
momentum profits.
Implementation translation:
- Regress each stock's daily or monthly returns on market/sector/factor returns over a lookback window.
- Compute residual cumulative return over the formation period.
- Rank on residual_momentum instead of total_return_momentum.
Useful S&P 500 algorithms:
- Residual 12-1 momentum after removing SPY and sector ETF exposure.
- Residual momentum + low-volatility weighting.
- Composite: total momentum only if residual momentum confirms.
Main risks:
- Regression window choices can overfit.
- Residual estimates are noisy for stocks with missing data.
- Factor set must be known only through historical data.

03. Jegadeesh (1990)
Title: Evidence of Predictable Behavior of Security Returns
Source: https://ideas.repec.org/a/bla/jfinan/v45y1990i3p881-98.html
Core idea:
Individual stock returns show negative first-order serial correlation at monthly horizons and
positive serial correlation at longer lags. This paper helps motivate both short-term reversal
and longer-horizon momentum.
Implementation translation:
- Feature: one_month_reversal = -return over last 21 trading days.
- Combine with longer-horizon momentum to avoid fighting strong trends.
Useful S&P 500 algorithms:
- Monthly reversal among liquid stocks: long prior-month losers, short prior-month winners.
- Reversal only after large idiosyncratic moves, neutralized for sector and beta.
- Momentum-minus-reversal composite: favor stocks with strong 12-1 momentum but weak last-month runup.
Main risks:
- Bid-ask bounce and stale pricing can fake reversal in less liquid universes.
- S&P 500 reversal may be small after costs.

04. Lehmann (1990)
Title: Fads, Martingales, and Market Efficiency
Source: https://www.nber.org/papers/w2533
Core idea:
Very short-term winners and losers, especially over weekly intervals, tend to reverse. The
paper interprets this as evidence of short-lived price pressure or liquidity effects.
Implementation translation:
- Feature: weekly_reversal = -return over prior 5 trading days.
- Use strict liquidity and spread filters.
- Use conservative slippage because expected edge can be small.
Useful S&P 500 algorithms:
- Weekly sector-neutral reversal on large S&P 500 constituents.
- Reversal after abnormal volume or abnormal one-week move.
- Reversal with no-trade bands to reduce turnover.
Main risks:
- High turnover.
- Implementation shortfall can eliminate edge.
- Hard to execute well without intraday/open-close modeling.

05. Heston and Sadka (2008)
Title: Seasonality in the Cross-Section of Stock Returns
Source: https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0304405X0700195X
Core idea:
Stocks tend to have relatively high or low returns in the same calendar month year after year.
This annual same-month autocorrelation pattern can persist across many annual lags.
Implementation translation:
- Feature: same_month_history = average return in this calendar month over prior N years.
- Rank stocks each month by historical same-month performance.
- Combine with momentum and liquidity filters.
Useful S&P 500 algorithms:
- Same-month seasonality rank, monthly rebalance.
- Seasonality-confirmed momentum: only buy momentum stocks with positive month-specific history.
- Avoid known seasonal losers during their historically weak months.
Main risks:
- Data mining risk is high.
- Needs long history and robust cross-validation.
- Calendar effects may decay after publication.

06. Cooper, Gulen, and Schill (2008)
Title: Asset Growth and the Cross-Section of Stock Returns
Source: https://ideas.repec.org/a/bla/jfinan/v63y2008i4p1609-1651.html
Core idea:
Firms with high total asset growth subsequently underperform firms with low asset growth.
The effect remains meaningful even after controlling for common predictors such as value,
size, momentum, and accruals.
Implementation translation:
- Feature: asset_growth = total_assets_t / total_assets_t_minus_1 - 1.
- Rank low asset growth as better.
- Combine with issuance, profitability, and valuation.
Useful S&P 500 algorithms:
- Long conservative asset-growth firms, avoid/short aggressive asset-growth firms.
- Penalize high asset growth in quality and value models.
- Asset growth + low accruals + high profitability composite.
Main risks:
- Sector effects are large.
- Acquisitive firms can have high asset growth for strategic reasons.
- Fundamental data must be point-in-time.

07. Pontiff and Woodgate (2008)
Title: Share Issuance and Cross-Sectional Returns
Source: https://paperzz.com/doc/9459249/share-issuance-and-cross-sectional-returns
Core idea:
Post-1970, share issuance strongly predicts lower future returns. The annual issuance measure
can be more statistically significant than size, value, or momentum as a cross-sectional predictor.
Implementation translation:
- Feature: net_share_issuance = change in split-adjusted shares outstanding over prior year.
- High issuance is negative; repurchase/shrinking share count is positive.
Useful S&P 500 algorithms:
- Long firms reducing shares outstanding, avoid firms issuing shares.
- Add net issuance penalty to value screens.
- Issuance-aware quality model: prefer profitable firms with buybacks funded by cash flow.
Main risks:
- Share count data must be split-adjusted.
- Issuance from compensation, M&A, or index events needs interpretation.

08. Loughran and Ritter (1995)
Title: The New Issues Puzzle
Source: https://ideas.repec.org/a/bla/jfinan/v50y1995i1p23-51.html
Core idea:
Companies issuing stock through IPOs or seasoned equity offerings historically underperform
over long horizons. The finding supports the idea that managers issue equity when valuations
are favorable to the firm.
Implementation translation:
- Feature: recent_equity_issue_flag, ipo_age, seo_flag, months_since_issue.
- For S&P 500, IPOs are rare, but seasoned issuance and recent inclusion after IPO can matter.
Useful S&P 500 algorithms:
- Avoid recent large equity issuers.
- Underweight recent IPOs/newly public firms until they season.
- Pair issuance flags with valuation and profitability to detect overvalued growth stories.
Main risks:
- S&P 500 inclusion often happens long after IPO.
- Event datasets must be complete and timestamped.

09. Ikenberry, Lakonishok, and Vermaelen (1995)
Title: Market Underreaction to Open Market Share Repurchases
Source: https://econpapers.repec.org/paper/nbrnberwo/4965.htm
Core idea:
Open-market repurchase announcements are followed by positive long-run abnormal returns,
especially for value stocks. Markets appear to underreact to the information conveyed by
repurchase announcements.
Implementation translation:
- Feature: repurchase_announcement_flag, buyback_authorization_size, value_status.
- Hold horizon can be long: 6 months to several years.
Useful S&P 500 algorithms:
- Long value stocks after buyback announcements.
- Shareholder yield strategy: buybacks + dividends + debt reduction.
- Repurchase confirmation: require actual share count reduction after authorization.
Main risks:
- Authorization does not guarantee actual repurchase.
- Buybacks can be poorly timed or debt-funded.
- Need announcement data and later share count confirmation.

10. Daniel and Titman (2006 / working paper versions 2001-2003)
Title: Market Reactions to Tangible and Intangible Information
Source: https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=414701
Core idea:
The return component not explained by tangible accounting performance, called intangible
information, can predict future returns. It separates price movement due to fundamentals
from price movement not obviously backed by accounting performance.
Implementation translation:
- Decompose past return into tangible component and residual/intangible component.
- Intangible return can be proxied by price return minus accounting-performance-implied return.
Useful S&P 500 algorithms:
- Fade large positive intangible returns when not backed by fundamentals.
- Buy negative intangible return stocks when fundamentals are stable.
- Combine with sentiment/attention measures to identify hype-driven moves.
Main risks:
- Decomposition is complex and sensitive to accounting definitions.
- May overlap with value/reversal/issuance effects.

11. Chan, Jegadeesh, and Lakonishok (1996)
Title: Momentum Strategies
Source: https://www.nber.org/papers/w5375
Core idea:
Past returns and past earnings surprises each predict future return drift even after controlling
for each other. Analyst earnings forecasts also respond sluggishly to past news.
Implementation translation:
- Features: price_momentum, standardized_unexpected_earnings, analyst_revision_lag.
- Build separate price-momentum and earnings-momentum signals.
Useful S&P 500 algorithms:
- Dual momentum: require both price momentum and earnings surprise momentum.
- Earnings momentum sleeve independent from price momentum.
- Avoid stocks with bad earnings momentum even if price momentum looks strong.
Main risks:
- Requires clean earnings surprise data and announcement timing.
- Price and earnings signals can conflict around regime shifts.

12. Womack (1996)
Title: Do Brokerage Analysts' Recommendations Have Investment Value?
Source: https://ideas.repec.org/a/bla/jfinan/v51y1996i1p137-67.html
Core idea:
Analyst recommendation changes produce immediate price reactions and post-event drift.
Sell recommendations had especially large and longer-lasting negative drift in the original study.
Implementation translation:
- Feature: recommendation_change, new_buy_flag, new_sell_flag, downgrade_magnitude.
- Trade after recommendation date/time, not before.
Useful S&P 500 algorithms:
- Long upgrades, avoid/short downgrades.
- Stronger signal for rare sell/downgrade events.
- Combine analyst changes with earnings revisions and momentum.
Main risks:
- Analyst recommendation data can be costly.
- Public dissemination timing matters.
- Recommendations may be biased or stale.

13. Jegadeesh, Kim, Krische, and Lee (2004)
Title: Analyzing the Analysts: When Do Recommendations Add Value?
Source: https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=291241
Core idea:
Consensus recommendation levels can be misleading because analysts often favor glamour stocks.
Recommendation changes are more robust predictors and add information beyond many quantitative variables.
Implementation translation:
- Prefer delta_consensus_recommendation over consensus_level.
- Interact analyst changes with value and momentum characteristics.
Useful S&P 500 algorithms:
- Buy positive recommendation changes only when valuation/momentum context is favorable.
- Penalize highly recommended but expensive/negative-momentum stocks.
- Short/avoid glamour stocks with deteriorating consensus.
Main risks:
- Consensus data revision history must be point-in-time.
- Analyst coverage is dense for S&P 500, which may reduce edge.

14. Lakonishok and Lee (2001)
Title: Are Insiders' Trades Informative?
Source: https://econpapers.repec.org/paper/nbrnberwo/6656.htm
Core idea:
Insiders tend to be contrarian and insider purchases are more informative than insider sales.
The predictive power is stronger in smaller firms, but insider buying can still be useful as
a confirmation signal in large caps.
Implementation translation:
- Features: net_insider_buying, insider_purchase_cluster, insider_role, dollar_value_purchased.
- Treat purchases as more informative than sales.
Useful S&P 500 algorithms:
- Insider-confirmed value: buy cheap stocks with clustered insider purchases.
- Avoid stocks with repeated insider selling plus negative fundamentals.
- Use insider buying as a filter, not a standalone daily signal.
Main risks:
- Planned sales and compensation-related transactions are noisy.
- SEC filing delays and amendments matter.
- S&P 500 insider signal may be weaker than small-cap signal.

15. Boehmer, Jones, and Zhang (2008)
Title: Which Shorts Are Informed?
Source: https://business.columbia.edu/faculty/research/which-shorts-are-informed
Core idea:
Short sellers are informative as a group; heavily shorted stocks underperform lightly shorted
stocks over following weeks. Institutional non-program short sales are especially informative.
Implementation translation:
- Features: short_sale_volume_ratio, short_interest_ratio, institutional_short_proxy.
- High informed shorting is negative.
Useful S&P 500 algorithms:
- Avoid high short-interest stocks unless offset by strong fundamentals and momentum.
- Short high shorting + negative earnings revision stocks.
- Use shorting pressure as a risk overlay on long portfolios.
Main risks:
- Proprietary NYSE short-sale data may not be available.
- Public short interest is delayed.
- High short interest can create squeeze risk.

16. Diether, Lee, and Werner (2009)
Title: Short-Sale Strategies and Return Predictability
Source: https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/rfinst/v22y2009i2p575-607.html
Core idea:
Short sellers increase activity after positive returns and predict future negative abnormal
returns. Their behavior is consistent with trading against short-term overreaction.
Implementation translation:
- Feature: abnormal_short_sale_activity after positive stock move.
- Use as a fast negative signal when data is timely.
Useful S&P 500 algorithms:
- Short/avoid stocks with abnormal shorting after price spikes.
- Combine short-sale activity with weekly reversal.
- Use short-sale data to improve sell timing for long-only strategies.
Main risks:
- Data availability and latency.
- Borrow costs and short squeeze risk.
- Daily signals can be very cost-sensitive.

17. Pan and Poteshman (2006)
Title: The Information in Option Volume for Future Stock Prices
Source: https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=1096000
Core idea:
Option trading volume contains information about future stock prices. Buyer-initiated put-call
ratios predict next-day and next-week stock returns, suggesting informed trading in options.
Implementation translation:
- Feature: option_put_call_opening_buy_ratio, or public proxy using put/call volume and open interest.
- Low put-call ratio is bullish; high put-call ratio is bearish.
Useful S&P 500 algorithms:
- Options-flow confirmation for earnings/momentum trades.
- Short-term bullish/bearish signal from abnormal call vs put buying.
- Avoid longs when option flow turns strongly bearish.
Main risks:
- True buyer-initiated opening volume is hard to obtain.
- Public option volume proxies are noisy.
- Signal horizon is short and cost-sensitive.

18. Cremers and Weinbaum (2010)
Title: Deviations from Put-Call Parity and Stock Returns
Source: https://chesler.us/resources/academia/Deviations%20from%20Put-Call%20Parity%20and%20Stock%20Returns.pdf
Core idea:
Deviations from put-call parity, often expressed through implied-volatility spreads between
calls and puts, contain information about future stock returns.
Implementation translation:
- Feature: call_put_iv_spread = ATM_call_IV - ATM_put_IV, maturity-matched.
- Positive spread can indicate bullish informed demand; negative spread can indicate bearish pressure.
Useful S&P 500 algorithms:
- Options-implied return signal for optionable S&P 500 names.
- Combine with Pan-Poteshman option-volume signal.
- Use put-call parity deviations as an event-confirmation filter.
Main risks:
- Requires clean option surfaces and dividend/borrow/rate assumptions.
- Mispricing can reflect frictions, not alpha.
- Must avoid stale or illiquid option contracts.

19. Baker and Wurgler (2006)
Title: Investor Sentiment and the Cross-Section of Stock Returns
Source: https://ideas.repec.org/p/nbr/nberwo/10449.html
Core idea:
Broad investor sentiment affects stocks that are hard to value or hard to arbitrage more strongly.
When sentiment is high, speculative/volatile/unprofitable/no-dividend/growth stocks tend to have
lower subsequent returns; when sentiment is low, these stocks can rebound.
Implementation translation:
- Features: sentiment_regime, sentiment_sensitive_score, speculative_growth_score.
- Use sentiment as a regime filter, not a standalone stock rank.
Useful S&P 500 algorithms:
- Reduce exposure to speculative growth during high-sentiment regimes.
- Increase contrarian exposure after sentiment collapses and trend stabilizes.
- Sentiment-aware short book: expensive, unprofitable, high-volatility names in high sentiment.
Main risks:
- Sentiment indices can be delayed and revised.
- Timing sentiment reversals is hard.
- High sentiment can persist for years.

20. Da, Engelberg, and Gao (2011)
Title: In Search of Attention
Source: https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=1364209
Core idea:
Google Search Volume Index provides a direct measure of investor attention. Search activity
is linked to retail investor behavior and can help explain IPO returns and momentum behavior.
Implementation translation:
- Feature: abnormal_search_volume = current ticker/company search volume relative to trailing baseline.
- Combine with volume, news, and retail-trading proxies.
Useful S&P 500 algorithms:
- Attention-confirmed momentum: momentum works better when attention supports it.
- Fade high-attention spikes when fundamentals/options/insider signals disagree.
- Avoid buying high-attention stocks after extreme retail-driven price jumps.
Main risks:
- Ticker search data can be ambiguous.
- Google Trends data is sampled/scaled and can be hard to reproduce.
- Attention is not always bearish; context matters.

Optional future extension: Aboody, Even-Tov, Lehavy, and Trueman (2018)
Title: Overnight Returns and Firm-Specific Investor Sentiment
Source: https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=2554010
Core idea:
Overnight returns can serve as a proxy for firm-specific investor sentiment. High overnight
returns show short-term persistence but longer-term underperformance, especially for harder-to-value firms.
Implementation translation:
- Feature: overnight_return = open_t / close_t_minus_1 - 1.
- Separate close-to-open and open-to-close returns.
- Use rolling average overnight return as sentiment proxy.
Useful S&P 500 algorithms:
- Fade persistent high overnight sentiment after confirmation of overpricing.
- Use overnight/intraday decomposition to distinguish retail sentiment from institutional trading.
- Avoid buying stocks whose momentum is entirely overnight-driven and unsupported by fundamentals.
Main risks:
- Requires reliable open prices and corporate action handling.
- Open auction liquidity/price quality matters.
- Overnight return can reflect real news, not only sentiment.

10 ADDITIONAL ALGORITHM DEVELOPMENT BATCHES

BATCH 1: Industry Momentum and Sector Rotation
Source papers:
- Moskowitz and Grinblatt (1999)
Objective:
Build industry-level momentum as a separate signal and as an overlay for stock momentum.
Algorithms:
1. Industry top-minus-bottom momentum: long top industries, short bottom industries.
2. Long-only sector rotation: allocate to top 3-5 S&P 500 industries or sectors.
3. Industry-confirmed stock momentum: buy stock momentum only when industry momentum is positive.
4. Industry-relative momentum: rank stocks by return relative to their industry.
Data needed:
- point-in-time GICS/industry mapping
- daily/monthly returns
- sector/industry ETFs for benchmarks
Validation:
- Compare to SPY, equal-sector S&P 500, and classic stock momentum.
- Watch sector concentration and turnover during rotations.

BATCH 2: Residual Momentum and Clean Momentum
Source papers:
- Blitz, Huij, and Martens (2011)
- Daniel and Titman (2006)
Objective:
Separate stock-specific momentum from market/sector/factor exposures.
Algorithms:
1. Residual momentum after SPY + sector ETF regression.
2. Residual momentum after Fama-French-style factor proxy regression.
3. Residual momentum with value/quality exclusions.
4. Intangible-return reversal: fade large non-fundamental price moves.
Data needed:
- stock returns
- SPY returns
- sector ETF returns
- optional factor returns or local factor proxies
Validation:
- Report alpha after market/sector/factor exposure.
- Compare residual momentum vs total momentum.

BATCH 3: Short-Term Reversal and Liquidity Pressure
Source papers:
- Jegadeesh (1990)
- Lehmann (1990)
Objective:
Exploit short-horizon reversal without letting costs eat the edge.
Algorithms:
1. Weekly reversal: long prior-week losers, short prior-week winners.
2. Monthly reversal: long prior-month losers, short prior-month winners.
3. Reversal after abnormal volume, sector-neutral.
4. Reversal with no-trade bands and ADV/slippage filters.
Data needed:
- daily returns
- volume/ADV
- spreads or spread proxy
- borrow/cost assumptions
Validation:
- Run pre-cost and post-cost.
- Sensitivity test slippage from 1 bps to 20 bps.
- Require capacity estimate.

BATCH 4: Seasonality and Calendar Memory
Source papers:
- Heston and Sadka (2008)
Objective:
Test annual same-month return memory as a low-frequency signal.
Algorithms:
1. Same-month seasonality rank.
2. Seasonality-confirmed momentum.
3. Seasonality as a position-size modifier.
4. Month-specific avoid list for historically weak stocks.
Data needed:
- long monthly return history
- point-in-time universe membership
Validation:
- Walk-forward only.
- Test across different lookback years: 5, 10, 15, 20.
- Use false discovery controls because calendar effects are data-mining prone.

BATCH 5: Corporate Issuance, Asset Growth, and Buyback Signals
Source papers:
- Cooper, Gulen, and Schill (2008)
- Pontiff and Woodgate (2008)
- Loughran and Ritter (1995)
- Ikenberry, Lakonishok, and Vermaelen (1995)
Objective:
Use corporate financing behavior as a slow-moving alpha signal.
Algorithms:
1. Conservative-growth portfolio: low asset growth + low net issuance.
2. Shareholder-yield portfolio: buybacks + dividends + low issuance.
3. Avoid recent issuers and high asset-growth firms.
4. Buyback-value drift: value stocks after repurchase announcements.
Data needed:
- shares outstanding
- fundamentals
- buyback announcements
- IPO/SEO/recent issuance flags
Validation:
- Quarterly/monthly rebalance only.
- Confirm actual share count reduction, not only authorization.
- Separate debt-funded buybacks from cash-flow-funded buybacks if possible.

BATCH 6: Earnings Momentum and Analyst Revision Signals
Source papers:
- Chan, Jegadeesh, and Lakonishok (1996)
- Womack (1996)
- Jegadeesh, Kim, Krische, and Lee (2004)
Objective:
Build event and revision signals from earnings news and analyst behavior.
Algorithms:
1. Earnings momentum: buy high earnings surprise and positive revision names.
2. Analyst upgrade/downgrade drift.
3. Consensus recommendation change, not level.
4. Analyst signal only when supported by value/momentum context.
Data needed:
- earnings announcement dates and surprises
- analyst recommendations
- consensus revisions
- price returns
Validation:
- Event-time backtest: 1, 5, 20, 60 trading-day horizons.
- Use public release timestamp plus execution delay.
- Compare large-cap vs mid-cap S&P 500 constituents.

BATCH 7: Insider and Short-Seller Information
Source papers:
- Lakonishok and Lee (2001)
- Boehmer, Jones, and Zhang (2008)
- Diether, Lee, and Werner (2009)
Objective:
Use informed-trader footprints as confirmation and risk-control signals.
Algorithms:
1. Insider-confirmed value: cheap stocks with clustered insider buying.
2. Short-pressure avoid list: avoid high short-interest/high short-sale-activity names.
3. Spike-and-short reversal: short-sale activity after positive price spikes.
4. Disagreement model: insider buying vs high short interest conflict monitor.
Data needed:
- Form 4 insider transactions
- short interest and/or short-sale activity
- borrow costs if shorting
- price/volume
Validation:
- Use filing dates, not transaction dates, unless trading timestamp is known.
- Run avoid/underweight implementation for long-only feasibility.
- Track short squeeze losses and borrow assumptions.

BATCH 8: Options-Informed Equity Signals
Source papers:
- Pan and Poteshman (2006)
- Cremers and Weinbaum (2010)
Objective:
Use options market information to improve short-term stock selection and risk filters.
Algorithms:
1. Put-call volume signal: bullish when call-buying dominates put-buying.
2. Put-call parity deviation signal.
3. Options-confirmed earnings drift.
4. Bearish options warning overlay for long portfolio.
Data needed:
- option volume by contract
- implied volatilities
- strikes, maturities, rates, dividends
- stock returns and event dates
Validation:
- Start with liquid optionable S&P 500 names only.
- Exclude stale/illiquid contracts.
- Test next-day, next-week, and next-month horizons separately.

BATCH 9: Attention, Sentiment, and Overnight Behavior
Source papers:
- Baker and Wurgler (2006)
- Da, Engelberg, and Gao (2011)
- Optional future extension: Aboody, Even-Tov, Lehavy, and Trueman (2018)
Objective:
Use attention and sentiment as regime filters and overpricing detectors.
Algorithms:
1. High-sentiment speculative-stock underweight.
2. Abnormal search-volume attention overlay.
3. Fade high-attention spikes when unsupported by fundamentals/options.
4. Overnight sentiment reversal: fade persistent high overnight sentiment in hard-to-value names.
Data needed:
- sentiment index or proxy
- Google Trends/search proxy
- news volume
- open/close prices
- firm difficulty-to-value proxies
Validation:
- Separate high-sentiment and low-sentiment regimes.
- Test whether attention confirms momentum or predicts reversal.
- Beware ticker ambiguity in search data.

BATCH 10: Set 2 Ensemble and Signal Conflict Engine
Source papers:
- All Set 2 papers
Objective:
Combine specialized signals into a transparent ensemble with conflict handling.
Algorithms:
1. Signal family scores: industry, residual momentum, reversal, corporate action, analyst, insider/short, options, attention.
2. Conflict-aware ranking: downgrade names where informed sellers oppose price momentum.
3. Event horizon allocator: use different holding periods by signal type.
4. Long-only avoid engine: improve a baseline long portfolio by removing red-flag stocks.
Data needed:
- all feature families, but allow missing modules
- standardized daily/monthly feature store
Validation:
- Compare ensemble to each sleeve and to equal-weighted sleeve blend.
- Use walk-forward weights or fixed equal weights first.
- Track contribution by sleeve, turnover, and drawdown.

   Reason: use as overlay after baseline signals exist.
