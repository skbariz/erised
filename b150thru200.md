# H150–H200: S&P 500 Cross-Sectional Stock Selection Strategy Research Plan
# Synthesis of 20 Academic Papers + H001–H100 Program Learnings

---

## EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

**Program focus.** H150–H200 is exclusively a stock-selection program: every strategy ranks the ~500 members of the S&P 500 by signals derived from price history, fundamental data, and events, then holds the highest-ranked stocks (and optionally shorts the lowest-ranked). All strategies trade individual S&P 500 constituent stocks using point-in-time membership to avoid survivorship bias.

This is entirely different from H001–H100, which expressed views on *when* to be in the market via ETF asset allocation. H150–H200 expresses views on *which* stocks to hold within equities — the other axis of equity alpha.

**Why S&P 500 stock selection.** S&P 500 stocks are liquid, well-researched, and have reliable price histories. The large-cap universe avoids illiquidity costs that plague small-cap factor strategies. Point-in-time membership is tractable to reconstruct. The universe of ~500–1100 historical constituents gives sufficient cross-section for robust ranking and diversification.

**Target outcome.** A cross-sectional MDR ensemble of the best 10–20 kept H150–H199 strategies, targeting Sharpe 1.2–1.5, Max DD < −15%, at the S&P 500 stock portfolio level.

## PART I: H001–H100 LEARNINGS APPLIED TO H150–H200

### Critical lesson 1: Long-only first
Every long/short strategy in H001–H100 failed or underperformed its long-only equivalent. H013 (MA L/S) died vs H001 (MA long-only). H014 (momentum L/S) died vs H007. The culprit: 1.5 bps/turn costs on the short leg, with insufficient bear-market alpha to compensate. For individual stocks the same economics apply — short borrow costs run 50–200 bps/year for average S&P 500 names, and market-neutral strategies require 2× capital. **Rule: implement long-only first for every batch. Add a short leg only if the long-only strategy is kept and the mechanism is demonstrably non-symmetric.**

### Critical lesson 2: Vol-targeting is the primary risk lever
H086 (LowVolTargetPortfolio) improved H021's Sharpe by +0.080 and reduced Max DD by 8.48 percentage points *simultaneously* — the only modification in the program that improved both metrics at once. The mechanism is simple: H021 weights × min(1, target_vol / realized_vol). Apply the same overlay to every H150–H200 strategy sleeve. A long-only momentum portfolio with a 10% annualized vol target will outperform the raw unscaled version on both Sharpe and Max DD in expectation.

### Critical lesson 3: Trade count floor
Strategies with N < 50 OOS trades fail the confidence interval test regardless of signal quality. Contrarian strategies (H038–H042) all had this problem: H001 correlations as low as +0.108 but too few trades for reliable inference. Monthly-rebalanced portfolios of 50 stocks generate ~600 position-changes per year (50 stocks × 12 months) — well above the floor. **Minimum: 50 distinct OOS holding periods. Monthly rebalance with 20–50 stock portfolios is the natural operating frequency.**

### Critical lesson 4: CI lower bound > 0 is the hard keep bar
Not Sharpe alone. The 95% confidence interval lower bound must be positive. Strategies that show Sharpe 0.55 but CI [−0.05, 1.15] are killed — the wide CI reflects either too few trades or too much fold variance. Fold variance is signal for overfitting or regime-instability.

### Critical lesson 5: Ensemble via MDR is the terminal step
H095 (MaxDiversificationPortfolio) beat every simpler ensemble method by constructing weights that maximize the ratio of portfolio volatility to weighted-average component volatility. The same SLSQP MDR optimizer should be applied in H200 across all kept H150–H200 strategies, and then in H201+ for a cross-program MDR combining H001–H100 kept strategies with H150–H200 kept strategies.

### Critical lesson 6: Sensitivity analysis is mandatory for kept strategies
Every kept strategy in H001–H100 required a 1-D parameter sweep before finalization. For cross-sectional strategies the key parameters are: formation window (e.g., 3m vs 6m vs 12m momentum), holding period (monthly vs weekly), portfolio size (top 20 vs top 50 names), and weighting scheme (equal weight vs inverse vol weight). Robustness across these dimensions distinguishes a real signal from an in-sample fit.

### Critical lesson 7: Equity factors add complexity without proportional Sharpe gain
H061–H065 (equity factor ETF strategies) all had high H001 correlations (+0.64–0.95) and modest Sharpe (0.47–0.58). The lesson: factor ETFs are too blended and too correlated with SPY to produce genuine decorrelation. Individual stock selection is where factor signals have their edge — this is the architecture H150–H200 uses.

## PART II: INFRASTRUCTURE REQUIREMENTS

H150–H200 requires a different infrastructure than H001–H100. This section describes what must be built before implementation begins.

### 2.1 Data pipeline (new)

**Individual stock price data.**
- Daily adjusted OHLCV and total returns for all S&P 500 constituent members from 1993–2026.
- Corporate actions (splits, dividends, ticker changes, mergers, delistings) must be applied correctly.
- Recommended source: CRSP (academic), Sharadar/Norgate (retail), or Tiingo bulk download.

**Point-in-time S&P 500 membership.**
- A table with columns: (date, ticker, add_date, remove_date).
- Critical: use the membership as of the signal computation date, not today's membership. Survivorship bias inflates cross-sectional strategies by 1–3% CAGR on average.
- The backtest must only rank stocks that were in the index at the time of signal computation.
- Recommended source: Tiingo (has historical S&P 500 constituents), or manual reconstruction from SEC/Compustat.

**Quarterly fundamental data.**
- Availability date: use the SEC filing date (10-Q/10-K filed date), NOT the period end date. Typical lag is 45–75 days after quarter end. Using period-end date introduces lookahead bias.
- Required fields: total assets, book equity, net income, gross profit, revenue, COGS, total debt, cash, capex, shares outstanding, dividends paid.
- Recommended source: Compustat (via WRDS), Sharadar SFPY, or Polygon.io fundamentals.

**Earnings announcement calendar.**
- Timestamp of earnings announcement: must be before-market or after-market, with time precision.
- Use next-open execution (the day after the announcement) to avoid lookahead.
- Recommended source: Compustat I/B/E/S, Refinitiv, or Earnings Whispers.

### 2.2 Cross-sectional backtest engine (new module)

The existing engine.py handles single-position strategies (fraction of portfolio in SPY). H150–H200 requires a panel engine.

Minimum interface:
```python
class CrossSectionalEngine:
    def run(
        self,
        prices: pd.DataFrame,         # rows=dates, cols=tickers, values=adj_close
        weights: pd.DataFrame,         # rows=dates, cols=tickers, values=target_weight
        costs_bps: float = 2.5,        # slippage + commission per turn
    ) -> pd.Series:                    # daily portfolio returns
```

Key constraints:
- Weights must be computed using only data available before the execution date (1-day signal lag).
- Sum of |weights| ≤ 1.0 for long-only; = 2.0 gross for market-neutral.
- Rebalancing costs applied on position changes: |Δweight| × costs_bps.

### 2.3 Walk-forward protocol (adapted)

Same fold structure as H001–H100: 5-year train / 2-year test, 12 folds, 1993–2022.
But cross-sectional walk-forward operates differently:
- Train period: fit parameters (lookback windows, portfolio size thresholds) on in-sample data.
- Test period: apply fixed parameters OOS — no refitting.
- For ML strategies (H195–H199): expanding train window (all data before the test fold), not rolling.

### 2.4 Cost model for individual stocks

The H001–H100 cost model (0.5 bps commission + 1.0 bps slippage = 1.5 bps/turn) was for ETFs. Individual S&P 500 stocks are more liquid than average but less liquid than SPY.

**Conservative baseline: 2.0 bps slippage + 0.5 bps commission = 2.5 bps/turn.**
- Apply to every entry and exit.
- Monthly rebalance of 50-stock portfolio: ~600 trades/year × 2.5 bps = 150 bps/year cost drag.
- This is the primary reason many academic cross-sectional strategies degrade in live trading.
- Strategies must pass the keep bar *after* 2.5 bps/turn costs. Always report pre-cost and post-cost Sharpe.

SPY benchmark for H150–H200 is the same: Sharpe 0.51, CAGR 8.35%, Max DD −55.92% (same OOS windows).

## BATCH 1: Baseline Cross-Sectional Momentum (H150–H154)
**Papers: Jegadeesh & Titman (1993), George & Hwang (2004), Moskowitz, Ooi & Pedersen (2012)**

**Thesis.** The 12-1 month momentum anomaly (Jegadeesh & Titman) is the most replicated factor in the literature. Before adding any complexity, establish a clean baseline with long-only and long/short variants. These strategies are inherently stock-selection—they are NOT the same as H001's market-level time-series momentum. Cross-sectional momentum is long the top decile of S&P 500 stocks by past return while being flat (or short) the bottom decile.

**H001–H100 connection.** H007 (12-1m time-series momentum on SPY, Sharpe 0.62) and H001 (MA crossover, Sharpe 0.61) are market-level trend strategies — they go long or flat the entire index. H150–H154 operate within the index: the portfolio is always invested in individual S&P 500 stocks, not the index itself. Expected H001 correlation: +0.60–0.80 for long-only (all equity exposure); much lower for market-neutral L/S.

### H150: Cross-Sectional 12-1 Momentum, Long-Only Equal Weight

**Hypothesis.** Among S&P 500 stocks, the top quintile by 12-1 month return continues to outperform the bottom quintile over the next month. Long the top quintile (top 100 names), equal-weighted, rebalanced monthly. This is the Jegadeesh-Titman (1993) baseline applied to the point-in-time S&P 500 universe.

**Signal.** momentum_12_1 = price(t−21) / price(t−252) − 1. Rank descending. Long top quintile (~100 stocks). Rebalance first trading day of each month.

**Key regime expectations.**
- 2000–2002: Tech bubble peak-to-crash. Momentum will buy fallen tech leaders late (momentum crash risk). Expect fold 0–1 underperformance.
- 2008–2009: Momentum held financials into the crash, then missed the rebound. Expect fold 3 loss.
- 2009 rebound: Classic momentum crash — after March 2009 bottom, prior losers (financials) rallied 100%+ while prior winners lagged.
- 2020 COVID: Brief momentum crash (Feb–Apr 2020), recovery by year end.
- 2022: Growth stocks led during 2021; momentum held them into the 2022 bear. Crash risk.

**Success criteria.** OOS Sharpe > 0.60 (modest hurdle for baseline). If Sharpe < 0.51, momentum does not survive costs in this universe at monthly rebalance — do not continue momentum variations.
**Falsification.** Post-cost Sharpe < 0.51 → momentum is arbitraged/too costly in large-cap S&P 500.

**Implementation notes.**
- Strict point-in-time membership: rank only stocks that were in the index on the signal date.
- Skip months where fewer than 50 stocks have 252 days of return history (early folds).
- Apply 2.5 bps/turn cost on the ~20% turnover (stocks entering/leaving the top quintile monthly).
- Compare equal weight vs inverse-vol weight in sensitivity.

### H151: Cross-Sectional 12-1 Momentum, Market-Neutral Long/Short

**Hypothesis.** Long top quintile, short bottom quintile, beta-hedged. If pure cross-sectional momentum has alpha after costs, the long/short version captures it with lower market exposure. Market-neutral version has expected H001 corr ≈ 0 by construction.

**Signal.** Same as H150. Long top quintile (+2.5% per stock, ~40 stocks at 2× gross exposure = 1.0 long + 1.0 short = 2.0 gross). Short bottom quintile.

**H001–H100 application.** Every L/S strategy in H001–H100 failed. But that was market-level — shorting the entire index adds costs without bear-market alpha because the index has a positive drift (~8% CAGR). Cross-sectional L/S is different: you're shorting the *underperformers* within equities, not the entire equity market. The expected return differential between top and bottom quintile is ~4–6% before costs, which is not available at the market level.

**Expected H001 corr.** +0.05 to +0.25 (long and short both equity → partial cancellation; residual corr from beta exposure).
**Falsification.** Post-borrow-cost Sharpe < 0.30 → transaction costs and short borrow eliminate the cross-sectional edge in this universe.

**Implementation notes.**
- Beta-hedge by adjusting long/short weights to achieve net beta ≈ 0 (compute each stock's 1-year rolling beta to SPY, sum-weight betas to zero).
- Assume 50 bps/year borrow cost on the short leg.
- Short leg rebalance turnover is higher (bottom quintile is more volatile) — apply 3.0 bps/turn on short-side rebalancing.

### H152: 52-Week High Momentum (George & Hwang 2004)

**Hypothesis.** The ratio of current price to its 52-week high captures an anchoring effect: stocks near their yearly high have positive momentum and continue outperforming. This signal is distinct from 12-1 momentum in that it emphasizes *proximity to a price anchor*, not just past return.

**Signal.** high_52w_ratio = close(t) / max(close, 252 days). Rank descending. Long top quintile (~100 names). Rebalance monthly.

**Key distinction from H150.** A stock can have high 12-1 momentum but be far from its 52-week high (if it crashed and partially recovered). Conversely, a stock near its 52w-high may have modest 12-1 return (slow steady climber). The two signals are correlated (~0.60–0.70) but not identical.

**Success criteria.** OOS Sharpe > 0.55. If Sharpe < H150 (12-1 momentum), the 52w-high ratio adds no incremental value.
**Falsification.** Sharpe ≤ H150 → the anchoring interpretation adds no value over standard momentum.

**Implementation notes.**
- Test both rolling 252-day high and calendar-year high (resets January 1).
- Sensitivity: top quintile vs top 30% vs top decile.

### H153: Sector-Neutral Momentum

**Hypothesis.** Raw cross-sectional momentum has strong sector concentration: a sector in favor (e.g., energy in 2022, tech in 2020) dominates the top quintile. Sector-neutral momentum — ranking stocks within each of the 11 GICS sectors — selects the relative winners within each sector, removing sector concentration and reducing crash risk during sector reversals.

**Signal.** Within each of the 11 GICS sectors, rank stocks by 12-1 momentum. Select top 30% within each sector (approximately 10–15 names per sector). Equal-weight the resulting ~110-stock portfolio.

**H001–H100 application.** H046 (Halloween + momentum, Sharpe 0.707) demonstrated that conditioning momentum on a regime (seasonal window) significantly improves risk-adjusted returns. Sector conditioning is analogous — it removes the macro regime from the momentum signal and isolates the stock-specific component.

**Expected outcome.** Lower crash risk than H150 (no single-sector blow-up risk). Potentially slightly lower Sharpe in trending regimes (because the sector constraint filters out the strongest trend), but better Max DD and fold consistency.

### H154: SPY Trend-Gated Cross-Sectional Momentum

**Hypothesis.** Cross-sectional momentum crashes when the market reverses sharply (e.g., March 2009). The Moskowitz, Ooi & Pedersen (2012) time-series momentum result suggests that market-level trend filters the environment for cross-sectional strategies. When SPY is in a downtrend (below 200-day MA), reduce the long-only momentum portfolio to 50% (or zero) and hold the rest in T-bills. Resume full momentum exposure when SPY recovers above the 200MA.

**Signal.** H150 momentum signal. Exposure multiplier: 1.0 when SPY > 200dMA; 0.5 when SPY 100dMA > SPY > 200dMA; 0.0 when SPY < 200dMA.

**H001–H100 application.** H001 (MA crossover, Sharpe 0.61) vs H007 (12-1m momentum, Sharpe 0.62): adding the MA filter (H001) to TS momentum doesn't improve Sharpe at market level. But for cross-sectional long-only, the MA filter prevents holding the top-momentum stocks during bear markets (where momentum crashes occur). Expected improvement: +0.05–0.15 Sharpe over H150.

**Expected H001 corr.** +0.60–0.75 (the SPY MA filter introduces direct H001-like behavior). This strategy is explicitly a hybrid of market timing and stock selection.

## BATCH 2: Smooth Momentum and Crash-Managed Momentum (H155–H159)
**Papers: Da, Gurun & Warachka (2014), Barroso & Santa-Clara (2015), Moreira & Muir (2017)**

**Thesis.** Raw 12-1 momentum has a well-documented flaw: it loads heavily on recently-reversed stocks (stocks that crashed earlier in the window are ranked low, but if they've started recovering, they'll shoot up — catching the momentum portfolio short-covered). Smooth momentum (Da et al.) and volatility-scaled momentum (Barroso & Santa-Clara, Moreira & Muir) both address this. These papers directly apply Moreira & Muir's insight that H086 (LowVolTargetPortfolio) used at the portfolio level — extended here to individual stock momentum signals.

**H001–H100 application.** H086 proved that vol-targeting applied to H021's weights improved both Sharpe (+0.080) and Max DD (−8.48pp) simultaneously. The same principle applies to a momentum portfolio: reduce exposure to the momentum sleeve when the momentum portfolio's realized volatility is high. This is Barroso & Santa-Clara (2015) applied directly.

---

### H155: Smooth Momentum (Path-Consistent Momentum)

**Hypothesis.** Stocks whose price path over the past 12 months was smooth (many small positive days) have more durable momentum than stocks whose gain came from a few large jumps (earnings gap, acquisition rumor, short squeeze). The "frog in the pan" effect (Da et al.): investors underreact more to continuous gradual information than to discrete events.

**Signal.** smooth_momentum = 12-1 return × fraction_positive_days_in_window. Rank descending. Long top quintile. Alternatively: rank by 12-1 return, then among the top quintile, prefer stocks with > 55% positive days over the formation window.

**Key difference from H150.** A stock up 40% in 12 months on a steady upward path ranks higher than a stock up 40% from two 20% gap-up days. The smooth-path stock is more likely to continue; the jumpy stock may already have priced in all information.

**Expected outcome.** Lower crash frequency than H150 (jumpy stocks are often in M&A or event-driven situations that reverse quickly). Modestly lower average Sharpe but better tail behavior.

### H156: Jump-Penalized Momentum

**Hypothesis.** Complement to H155: explicitly penalize momentum that was driven by extreme daily returns. Compute the 12-1 return with and without the top 5 daily returns removed. If the momentum signal depends heavily on those extreme days, the signal is fragile.

**Signal.** trimmed_momentum = 12-1 return excluding top 5 and bottom 5 daily returns over the formation window (i.e., momentum computed on the "trimmed" return path). Rank descending. Long top quintile.

**Implementation notes.** This signal naturally downweights post-earnings momentum (large gap-up days) and rewards steady trend momentum. It is complementary to H155 and should be tested both as a standalone and as a combination (smooth momentum AND trimmed momentum both required for top-quintile inclusion).

### H157: Volatility-Scaled Momentum Sleeve (Barroso & Santa-Clara)

**Hypothesis.** The momentum portfolio's own realized volatility is time-varying and predictable. When momentum-portfolio volatility is high (immediately after market crashes, when momentum reversals are most likely), reduce exposure. Scale is: target_vol / realized_vol_of_momentum_sleeve, capped at 1.5× and floored at 0.2×.

**Signal.** Compute the daily return of the H150 momentum portfolio (top quintile equal-weight). Estimate trailing 21-day realized volatility of this portfolio return series. Exposure multiplier = min(1.5, max(0.2, 12% / annualized_portfolio_vol)). Multiply all position weights by this multiplier.

**H001–H100 application.** This is *exactly* H086 (LowVolTargetPortfolio) applied to the momentum portfolio instead of H021. H086 used: H021_weights × min(1, 8%/realized_vol). H157 uses the same mechanism with a 12% vol target (momentum is inherently more volatile than H021 → higher target). Expected improvement over H150: +0.08–0.15 Sharpe, −5–10pp Max DD.

**Critical implementation detail.** The vol estimate must be lagged by 1 day (use yesterday's vol to determine today's position). No lookahead.

---

### H158: Momentum with Drawdown Circuit Breaker

**Hypothesis.** During momentum crashes (March 2009, August 2015), the momentum portfolio can lose 15–25% in weeks. A drawdown circuit breaker that reduces exposure when the momentum portfolio is in a significant drawdown — preventing the "doubling down on losers" effect — can limit tail losses.

**Signal.** H150 momentum portfolio. Track the portfolio's running drawdown from its trailing 252-day high. When drawdown < −10%: reduce exposure to 50%. When drawdown < −15%: reduce to 25%. Resume full exposure when drawdown recovers above −5%.

**H001–H100 application.** H087 (RatchetDrawdownProtection, Sharpe 1.010, Max DD −10.93%) applied exactly this mechanism to H021: cut exposure proportional to portfolio drawdown depth. It improved Sharpe by −0.019 and Max DD by −7.48pp over H021. Expected similar benefit for the momentum portfolio.

### H159: Momentum × Vol-Target Composite

**Hypothesis.** Combine H155 (smooth momentum signal for stock selection) and H157 (vol-targeting for portfolio-level exposure management). This addresses two separate failure modes simultaneously: bad stock selection (jumpy momentum) and bad timing (high-vol periods).

**Signal.** Build the portfolio using smooth momentum ranks (H155 signal) AND scale total exposure by H157's vol target multiplier. This is the full Barroso & Santa-Clara + Da et al. combination.

**Expected outcome.** Best risk-adjusted outcome in Batch 1–2. The combination of signal quality improvement (smooth path) and exposure management (vol target) should deliver the highest Sharpe in the momentum family, estimated 0.75–0.95.

## BATCH 3: Value, Contrarian, and Reversal (H160–H164)
**Papers: Fama & French (1993), De Bondt & Thaler (1985), Asness, Moskowitz & Pedersen (2013)**

**Thesis.** Value (buying cheap stocks) and momentum are the two most robust cross-sectional factors in the literature. Value and momentum are also typically negatively correlated — value stocks are often past losers, while momentum picks past winners. Their combination (Asness, Moskowitz & Pedersen 2013) produces diversification benefits. The De Bondt–Thaler long-term reversal effect is the anti-momentum at 3–5 year horizons — a separate phenomenon from the 12-month continuation.

**H001–H100 application.** The program tested value via equity factor ETFs (H061–H065) and found all killed or modestly kept. The critical difference here: ETF-level value exposure (IVE = large-cap value ETF) provides a blended, already-diversified value tilt. Stock-level value selection concentrates on the most attractively valued names and should capture more alpha. But the program learned: equity factors add high H001 correlation (+0.67–0.95). The value + momentum composite (which is long-only) will also have high H001 corr. Accept this: the value of these strategies is their long-run Sharpe, not low correlation with H001.

### H160: Large-Cap Value (Book-to-Market + Earnings Yield)

**Hypothesis.** Within the S&P 500, stocks with high book-to-market (low P/B) and high earnings yield (high E/P) are systematically undervalued by the market and deliver excess returns over the next 6–12 months. Fama & French (1993) demonstrated this in a broad universe; the effect persists in large-cap names though it is more modest.

**Signal.** composite_value = 0.5 × rank(book_to_market) + 0.5 × rank(earnings_yield). Rank descending. Long top quintile (~100 names). Rebalance quarterly (fundamental data updates quarterly).

**Key risks (from paper).**
- Financial sector: banks have book value driven by loan portfolio quality, not replacement cost. Consider excluding financials or treating them separately.
- Negative book equity: exclude (book-to-market undefined or negative).
- Value underperformed severely 2017–2021. Expect 2–3 weak folds in the post-2015 test windows.

**H001–H100 application.** Value is an annual-frequency signal — it should be updated only when new quarterly/annual filings arrive. The 90-day filing lag means signals are slow and turnover is low (~30–50% per year vs. 150%+ for momentum). Low turnover = lower cost drag. This is an advantage for value strategies.

**Success criteria.** OOS Sharpe > 0.52. If value cannot beat the SPY benchmark in the S&P 500 universe after costs, the Fama-French value premium has been arbitraged away in large caps.

### H161: Long-Term Reversal (De Bondt & Thaler 1985)

**Hypothesis.** Stocks that have been the worst performers over the prior 3–5 years tend to subsequently outperform, and prior winners underperform. This reversal is driven by investor overreaction — stocks fall too far on bad news and recover as fundamentals stabilize.

**Signal.** long_term_reversal = −1 × price_return(t−1260, t−252) (negative 5-year return, excluding recent 12 months to avoid mixing with intermediate momentum). Rank descending (largest losers first). Long top quintile, but ONLY if the stock also has: (1) positive 6-month price return (recovery trend starting), (2) net debt / EBITDA < 3 (not a financial distress case), and (3) current ratio > 1 (solvent).

**Why the quality filter matters.** De Bondt & Thaler bought all long-term losers. The problem: cheap = potentially distressed. In a large-cap index, adding quality filters removes the "value traps" (companies fundamentally broken, not just temporarily beaten up). This is the lesson from H001–H100's contrarian strategies: H038 (long-term reversal at market level) had H001 corr +0.165 but N=62 trades and 7 zero folds. At stock level, the quality filter ensures enough tradeable opportunities exist every period.

**Expected H001 corr.** +0.20–0.40 (long-term reversal is anti-momentum; it will partly offset the market's trend behavior).

### H162: Value + Momentum Composite (Asness, Moskowitz & Pedersen 2013)

**Hypothesis.** Value and momentum are both individually profitable and negatively correlated with each other. A 50/50 composite score — z-score of value rank plus z-score of momentum rank — should deliver higher Sharpe than either factor alone because the two factors partially offset each other's bad periods (value crashes in bull markets, momentum crashes in sharp reversals).

**Signal.** composite = 0.5 × z_score(book_to_market + earnings_yield) + 0.5 × z_score(12-1 momentum). Rank descending. Long top quintile (~100 names). Rebalance monthly (update momentum monthly, value on quarterly filing date).

**H001–H100 application.** H049 (min-corr ensemble, Sharpe 0.959) combined H021 + H046 + H019 + H035, all pairwise correlation < 0.50. The same diversification principle applies within the stock selection factor space: combining value (slow, anticyclical) with momentum (fast, procyclical) reduces factor-specific crash risk. Expected improvement over either factor alone: +0.05–0.15 Sharpe.

**Critical implementation.** Value and momentum have different signal frequencies. Update the composite every month, but for the value component, use the most recent available fundamental data (which may be 3–9 months old at the time of signal computation). Do not assume quarterly filings arrive the day after the quarter ends.

### H163: Sector-Neutral Value + Momentum

**Hypothesis.** Raw value picks tend to concentrate in sectors with structurally low P/B (financials, energy, utilities). Raw momentum tends to concentrate in whichever sector is currently leading. Neutralizing both signals within sectors removes these sector-level tilts and isolates the stock-specific component of each factor.

**Signal.** Within each of the 11 GICS sectors, compute composite value + momentum z-scores. Rank within sector. Long top 30% within each sector. Equal-weight by sector (equal sector risk, not equal stock risk).

**Expected outcome.** Lower volatility than H162 (sector neutralization reduces sector concentration risk). Potentially slightly lower Sharpe in strongly trending sectors. Better tail behavior.

### H164: Long-Term Reversal + Quality (De Bondt-Thaler Upgraded)

**Hypothesis.** Upgrade H161 by adding Piotroski-style quality filtering (discussed in Batch 4) as a requirement for entering the long-term reversal portfolio. Among the bottom quintile by 5-year return, only buy stocks with F-score ≥ 6 (fundamentally healthy). This combines the mean-reversion signal with a quality confirmation.

**Signal.** Step 1: identify bottom quintile by 5-year return (prior losers). Step 2: compute a simplified F-score (6 binary checks: positive net income, positive operating cash flow, positive ROA trend, declining leverage, improving current ratio, positive gross margin trend). Step 3: long only if F-score ≥ 6 AND 6-month return is positive (recovery trend). Rebalance quarterly.

**Expected trade count.** With quality filter + recovery requirement, expect ~20–40 qualifying stocks per quarter in a 500-stock universe. This may cause N < 50 OOS trade-periods — watch CI carefully.

## BATCH 4: Quality and Fundamental Strength (H165–H169)
**Papers: Piotroski (2000), Novy-Marx (2013), Quality Minus Junk (AQR), Sloan (1996)**

**Thesis.** Quality (profitability, financial strength, earnings quality) is one of the most durable factor premia. Novy-Marx (2013) showed gross profitability is remarkably simple and effective. Sloan (1996) showed earnings quality (cash vs. accrual earnings) adds information beyond simple profitability. The AQR "Quality Minus Junk" framework bundles multiple quality dimensions into a composite. Unlike momentum, quality is a low-turnover signal that survives higher transaction costs because positions are held for years.

**H001–H100 application.** No quality signals were tested in H001–H100 (the program only had ETF-level equity access). This entire batch is genuinely new territory.

---

### H165: Gross Profitability Rank (Novy-Marx 2013)

**Hypothesis.** Gross profit divided by total assets (GP/A) is a powerful predictor of stock returns. Companies with high gross profitability have durable pricing power and competitive moats that generate above-average returns on capital. Novy-Marx showed this signal is distinct from book-to-market and has the opposite loading on value — high-profitability firms are often expensive, not cheap.

**Signal.** gross_profitability = (revenue − COGS) / total_assets. Rank descending within sector (gross margins differ structurally across sectors). Long top quintile. Rebalance annually (updates with annual filings) or semi-annually if quarterly data available.

**Key risks.** Industry structure differences are large: software companies have 80% gross margins; grocery retailers have 25%. Sector neutralization is mandatory. Use GICS-sector-neutral ranking.

**Expected outcome.** Low turnover (~20–30% annually), low transaction costs, expected Sharpe 0.55–0.70. This is a long-horizon value-adjacent strategy.

### H166: Low Accruals Quality (Sloan 1996)

**Hypothesis.** Firms with low accounting accruals (earnings driven by cash flows, not accounting adjustments) have more persistent earnings and outperform high-accrual firms. The accrual anomaly: investors overprice earnings that are accounting-driven (channel stuffing, aggressive capitalization, favorable timing of expense recognition) and underprice genuine cash generation.

**Signal.** accruals = (net_income − operating_cash_flow) / average_total_assets. Rank ascending (low accruals = better). Long the bottom quintile by accruals (low accrual = high quality). Rebalance annually.

**Combined test.** Also test as a screen: apply accruals filter only within the already-selected momentum top quintile (H150) — buy momentum stocks with low accruals and avoid momentum stocks with high accruals. Expected: small Sharpe improvement over pure momentum (accruals filters out earnings-driven "artificial" momentum).

### H167: Piotroski F-Score Value Quality

**Hypothesis.** Among cheap stocks (top quintile by book-to-market), the Piotroski F-score separates financially healthy value stocks from value traps. The 9-point F-score uses three categories: profitability (ROA, operating cash flow, ROA trend), leverage/liquidity (debt, current ratio, dilution), and operating efficiency (gross margin, asset turnover). High F-score value stocks significantly outperform low F-score value stocks.

**Signal.** Step 1: select the top quintile of S&P 500 by book-to-market (cheap stocks). Step 2: compute F-score (0–9). Long only if F-score ≥ 7 (high quality among cheap stocks). Rebalance annually on fiscal year-end filing date + 45-day lag.

**H001–H100 application.** The program's contrarian strategies (H038–H042) had the signal direction correct but failed on trade count. Piotroski's insight is precisely about improving trade quality within a value screen — avoiding the distressed firms that drag down raw value returns. This is a very different mechanism from H038.

**Expected outcome.** Very low turnover (~15–25% annually). High per-trade Sharpe, but trade count may be low (top quintile = 100 names, then F-score ≥ 7 requirement reduces to ~30–50 names). Watch CI carefully.

### H168: Composite Quality Score (QMJ-Inspired)

**Hypothesis.** Build a multi-dimensional quality composite using the QMJ framework: (1) profitability (gross profit/assets, net income/equity, cash flow/equity), (2) growth (5-year change in profitability), (3) safety (beta, idiosyncratic volatility, leverage, interest coverage), (4) payout (dividend yield + buyback yield). High composite quality stocks have historically earned a premium over low-quality "junk" stocks.

**Signal.** quality_composite = equal-weight of sector-neutral z-scores for each of the 4 sub-categories. Rank descending. Long top quintile. Rebalance quarterly (profitability and growth update faster than once per year).

**Key risk.** Quality is a broad concept — overlapping sub-signals may create the appearance of a robust composite while actually fitting to one dominant underlying dimension (likely low beta). Diagnose this by checking which sub-component contributes most to the OOS return, and whether the composite beats each component in sensitivity analysis.

### H169: Quality-Value Composite (Low Accruals + Gross Profitability + Value)

**Hypothesis.** Novy-Marx showed gross profitability complements value (they are negatively correlated: profitable firms are often expensive). Sloan showed accruals qualify profitability (cash earnings are more persistent). Combining all three — value (book-to-market), quality (gross profitability), and earnings quality (accruals) — into a single composite should outperform any single factor.

**Signal.** quality_value = (1/3) × z_score(book_to_market) + (1/3) × z_score(gross_profitability) + (1/3) × z_score(−accruals). Rank descending within sector. Long top quintile. Rebalance semi-annually.

**Expected outcome.** This is the most academically robust signal in Batch 4. Expected Sharpe 0.65–0.85, low turnover, good factor diversification (value + quality components have different factor exposures). Low momentum loading means this strategy will perform well during momentum crashes.

## BATCH 5: Profitability, Investment, and Multi-Factor (H170–H174)
**Papers: Fama & French (2015), Novy-Marx (2013)**

**Thesis.** The Fama-French 5-factor model adds profitability (RMW: robust minus weak) and investment (CMA: conservative minus aggressive) to the original 3-factor model. Firms with robust profitability and conservative investment (low asset growth) earn above-average returns in the model. This batch tests these factors directly, builds the full FF5-inspired composite, and tests whether alpha survives factor-exposure controls.

**H001–H100 application.** No equivalent exists. This batch is entirely new.

---

### H170: Robust Profitability Long Portfolio (RMW Signal)

**Hypothesis.** Firms with robust operating profitability — measured as operating profit divided by book equity — earn excess returns over firms with weak profitability. This is the FF5 RMW factor applied in a long-only context: simply overweight the top quintile by operating profitability.

**Signal.** operating_profitability = (revenue − COGS − SGA − interest expense) / book_equity. Rank descending within sector. Long top quintile. Rebalance annually.

**Comparison to H165.** Novy-Marx's gross profitability (H165) uses gross profit/assets. FF5's operating profitability uses operating profit/equity. They are correlated (~0.60–0.70) but differ in the treatment of leverage and operating expenses. Test both and compare OOS.

---

### H171: Conservative Investment Long Portfolio (CMA Signal)

**Hypothesis.** Firms with low asset growth (conservative investment) earn higher returns than firms with aggressive asset growth. The investment effect is well-documented: companies that expand rapidly dilute existing shareholders, signal overconfidence, and often destroy value through empire-building. Low-growth firms return cash to shareholders and trade at lower multiples relative to fundamentals.

**Signal.** asset_growth = (total_assets_t − total_assets_{t-1}) / total_assets_{t-1}. Rank ascending (low growth = better). Long bottom quintile by asset growth. Rebalance annually.

**Key risk.** Asset growth is noisy: acquisitions, organic capex, and working capital changes all affect total assets. Consider using net-of-depreciation growth or industry-adjusted growth to remove sector-level differences.

---

### H172: Avoid Aggressive Investment + Weak Profitability (Quality Short Screen)

**Hypothesis.** The FF5 model's alpha comes from both ends: long robust profitability + conservative investment, SHORT weak profitability + aggressive investment. In a long-only framework, apply the short signal as a *screen*: exclude from the long portfolio any stock that is in the bottom quintile for BOTH profitability AND the top quintile for asset growth (the "junk" category). The portfolio is not shorter — it simply avoids the worst names.

**Signal.** Start with the broad S&P 500 universe. Exclude any stock where: operating_profitability < 30th percentile AND asset_growth > 70th percentile (within sector). Long the remaining ~350 names, equal-weighted. Rebalance annually.

**Comparison.** Compare to H170 (pure profitability tilt) and H165 (pure gross profitability). This strategy holds most of the index but removes the worst ~15% of names. Expected: modest Sharpe improvement with very low turnover.

---

### H173: FF5 Composite (Value + RMW + CMA + Low Beta + Momentum)

**Hypothesis.** The full Fama-French 5-factor model plus momentum (adding Carhart 1997) and low beta (Frazzini-Pedersen 2014) as a 7-factor composite stock selection model. Each factor is sector-neutral z-scored and combined with equal weights.

**Signal.** ff5_composite = (1/6) × z(book_to_market) + (1/6) × z(operating_profitability) + (1/6) × z(−asset_growth) + (1/6) × z(12-1 momentum) + (1/6) × z(−beta) + (1/6) × z(−accruals). Rank descending. Long top quintile. Rebalance monthly (momentum updates monthly; fundamental signals carry over until new data arrives).

**Expected outcome.** The most comprehensive long-only factor model in the H150–H200 program. Expected Sharpe 0.70–0.90. This is the "kitchen sink" model — it includes all the documented factors and should serve as the baseline for comparing whether Batch 10's ML model (H195–H199) actually adds value over this interpretable composite.

### H174: Factor-Neutral Alpha (Residual Return Prediction)

**Hypothesis.** A stock's next-period return can be decomposed into: (1) market beta exposure, (2) sector exposure, (3) known factor exposures (value, momentum, quality, size), and (4) idiosyncratic alpha. By explicitly residualizing against all known factors, we isolate the idiosyncratic component that is genuinely unexplained. Any persistent alpha in this residual suggests a novel signal.

**Signal.** Regress each stock's monthly return on: SPY return, sector ETF return, FF5 factor returns (computed from the stock universe itself). Use the rolling 60-month OLS residual as the "factor-neutral return." Rank stocks by the z-score of their rolling 3-month average factor-neutral return. Long top quintile.

**Purpose.** This strategy's primary use is as a control: if a new signal (e.g., earnings announcement) has high correlation with factor-neutral alpha, it's genuinely novel. If it correlates with known factors, it's factor redundancy. Also a real strategy test.

## BATCH 6: Low-Beta, Defensive, and Vol-Managed Exposure (H175–H179)
**Papers: Frazzini & Pedersen (2014), Moreira & Muir (2017), Quality Minus Junk**

**Thesis.** The Betting Against Beta (BAB) effect (Frazzini & Pedersen) is one of the most robust anomalies: low-beta stocks earn higher risk-adjusted returns than high-beta stocks. The explanation: leverage-constrained investors overpay for high-beta stocks. In a long-only S&P 500 context, this means tilting toward low-beta, high-quality names should improve Sharpe. Combined with Moreira-Muir vol targeting (which H086 proved so effective), this batch builds the most defensively structured equity strategies in H150–H200.

**H001–H100 application.** H086 (LowVolTargetPortfolio, Sharpe 1.109) applied vol targeting to H021. H070 (Multi-asset dynamic vol targeting, Sharpe 1.066) extended it to a multi-asset context. Both proved the mechanism. H175–H179 extend vol targeting to cross-sectional stock selection. Additionally, H063 (Defensive sector overlay, kept) and H065 (Equal-weight defensive factor, kept) both demonstrated that defensive tilts within equity improve Sharpe modestly. H175–H179 do this more precisely at the individual stock level.

---

### H175: Low-Beta Quality Long Portfolio

**Hypothesis.** Long the top quintile of S&P 500 stocks ranked by a combination of: low beta (rolling 1-year daily beta to SPY) and high quality (composite from H168). Low-beta quality stocks are the "safe" compounders — businesses with durable earnings, low leverage, and low market sensitivity. They underperform in speculative bull markets but protect capital in downturns.

**Signal.** lbq_score = 0.5 × z_score(−rolling_252d_beta) + 0.5 × z_score(quality_composite). Rank descending within sector. Long top quintile. Rebalance monthly (beta updates daily; quality updates quarterly).

**Expected outcome.** Sharpe 0.60–0.80. Max DD < −30% (lower than raw SPY due to low-beta composition). H001 corr: +0.55–0.75 (still all equity, but low-beta component creates some divergence during market moves).

**Key regime test.** 2013–2017 low-vol bull market: low-beta significantly underperforms. Expect weaker folds here. 2008–2009: low-beta stocks lose less (they own defensive compounders, not leveraged cyclicals). 2022 rate rise: low-beta stocks may still hold growth-multiple stocks (utilities, staples) that were hit by rate rises.

---

### H176: BAB-Inspired Long/Short (Long Low-Beta, Short High-Beta)

**Hypothesis.** Long the bottom quintile by beta (low-beta stocks), short the top quintile by beta (high-beta stocks). Beta-neutral: scale weights so that long-side beta equals short-side beta, achieving zero market beta exposure. This replicates the Frazzini-Pedersen (2014) BAB factor in the S&P 500 universe.

**Signal.** Compute rolling 252-day daily beta for each stock. Long the bottom quintile (beta < 0.6 approximately). Short the top quintile (beta > 1.4 approximately). Scale to beta-neutral: multiply long weights by 1/avg_long_beta, multiply short weights by 1/avg_short_beta, then normalize to 2× gross exposure.

**H001–H100 application.** All L/S strategies in H001–H100 failed. BUT: H176 is fundamentally different. H001–H100 L/S was shorting the *market*. H176 is *within-market* L/S, which should have near-zero market exposure by design. The cost concern (borrow, turnover) remains. Apply 3.0 bps/turn on short side + 50 bps/year borrow.

**Falsification.** If borrow costs eliminate the edge: Sharpe < 0.30 after full costs → BAB has been arbitraged in large-cap space.

---

### H177: Vol-Managed Equity Portfolio (Moreira & Muir 2017)

**Hypothesis.** The Moreira-Muir insight: factor volatility varies more than factor expected return. Therefore, scaling factor exposure inversely to its recent realized volatility improves Sharpe. Apply this to the H173 FF5 composite portfolio: when the portfolio's realized vol is high, reduce total exposure toward cash; when realized vol is low, run at full exposure.

**Signal.** Build the H173 FF5 composite portfolio. Compute its trailing 21-day realized daily volatility. Exposure multiplier = min(1.0, 12%_target / annualized_portfolio_vol). Scale all weights by this multiplier; hold the remainder in T-bills.

**H001–H100 application.** This is H086 applied to the FF5 composite instead of H021. H086 improved both Sharpe (+0.080) and Max DD (−8.48pp) over H021. Expected similar improvement over H173.

---

### H178: Defensive Factor Blend (Quality + Low-Beta + Low Idiosyncratic Vol)

**Hypothesis.** Three defensive signals combined: quality (H168), low beta (H176 long leg), and low idiosyncratic volatility (residual volatility after removing market/sector beta). Stocks with high quality, low market sensitivity, AND low specific risk are the most defensive names in the S&P 500. They compound quietly without dramatic drawdowns.

**Signal.** defensive_blend = (1/3) × z(quality_composite) + (1/3) × z(−beta) + (1/3) × z(−idiosyncratic_vol). Rank descending within sector. Long top quintile. Rebalance monthly.

**Note.** Idiosyncratic vol = standard deviation of residuals from a rolling 252-day regression of stock return on SPY return. This removes market sensitivity and measures stock-specific risk.

---

### H179: Vol-Targeted Low-Beta Quality (H175 + H157 Combined)

**Hypothesis.** The full defensive architecture: use H175's low-beta quality signal for stock selection AND apply H157's vol-targeting overlay for portfolio-level exposure management. Both mechanisms protect capital — one at the stock level (select defensive names), one at the portfolio level (reduce total exposure in high-vol regimes).

**Signal.** Build H175 portfolio (low-beta quality long). Compute portfolio's 21-day realized vol. Apply multiplier = min(1.0, 10%_target / portfolio_vol). Scale weights. This is the cross-sectional analog of H086.

**Expected outcome.** This is the single most defensively positioned equity strategy in H150–H200. Expected Sharpe 0.65–0.85, Max DD < −20%, stable fold-by-fold performance. The combination of security selection (low-beta quality) and portfolio-level vol targeting is theoretically the most robust approach.

## BATCH 7: Earnings Drift and Event Signals (H180–H184)
**Papers: Jegadeesh & Livnat (2006), Sloan (1996), Piotroski (2000)**

**Thesis.** Post-earnings announcement drift (PEAD) is one of the most documented anomalies: after a positive earnings surprise, stocks continue drifting higher for 30–60 days as information slowly diffuses. Jegadeesh & Livnat strengthened this by showing that revenue-confirmed earnings surprises (both EPS and revenue beat) have stronger continuation than expense-driven beats. This batch tests event-driven strategies that exploit post-announcement price drift.

**H001–H100 application.** H047 (Pre-earnings drift, Sharpe 0.234) was killed — the 5-day pre-earnings window is too short for the drift to materialize and too brief to accumulate sufficient OOS observations. H180–H184 use the *post-announcement* drift with 20–60 day holding periods, which generates substantially more observations (4 earnings seasons × 500 stocks = ~2000 events per year).

**Critical implementation note.** Earnings timestamps must be correct. If a company announces earnings after market close on day T, the next executable price is day T+1 open. Using same-day execution on an after-market announcement is a lookahead bias that inflates results by capturing the gap-up/down itself, not the subsequent drift.

---

### H180: Revenue-Confirmed Earnings Drift (PEAD)

**Hypothesis.** Long stocks that beat both earnings AND revenue estimates (Jegadeesh & Livnat). The dual confirmation signal (both top line and bottom line positive surprise) is more credible than an EPS beat achieved through cost cuts or one-time items. Hold for 45 trading days after the announcement.

**Signal.** On each announcement date (properly lagged): if EPS_surprise > 0 AND revenue_surprise > 0 (both above consensus), add the stock to the long portfolio. Hold for 45 trading days. Exit regardless of price (time-based exit). Each stock is sized equally; new announcements enter, old positions exit.

**Expected trade count.** ~500 stocks × 4 quarters × 50% hit rate (double-beat) = ~1000 long positions per year. This is a high-frequency event strategy with sufficient OOS observations.

**Key risk.** PEAD may be weaker in mega-cap S&P 500 stocks due to analyst coverage density. Split results by market cap quintile and report separately.

---

### H181: Negative Earnings Drift (Avoid or Short)

**Hypothesis.** The symmetric downside: stocks that miss both EPS AND revenue (double-miss) continue declining for 30–60 days post-announcement. Long-only implementation: exit positions in stocks that double-miss (remove from all strategy portfolios for 45 days). Long/short implementation: short the double-miss stocks.

**Signal.** Avoidance filter applied to H180's long portfolio: if a held stock announces a double-miss while in the portfolio, exit immediately (next open) rather than waiting for the time-based exit. Also test a standalone short of all double-miss stocks.

**H001–H100 application.** This is an event-driven exit signal that improves any stock-selection strategy's return by avoiding the post-earnings disaster. Test it as a modification to H173 (FF5 composite) first.

---

### H182: Earnings Gap Continuation

**Hypothesis.** The announcement-day gap (close-to-open price move on earnings day) is itself a signal for subsequent drift: large positive gaps (> +3%) continue drifting higher for 10–20 days post-gap; large negative gaps continue drifting lower. This is separate from the EPS/revenue surprise signal — it captures the market's revealed interpretation of earnings quality.

**Signal.** For each earnings announcement: compute gap = (next_open / prior_close) − 1. If gap > +3%, add to long portfolio for 15 trading days. If gap < −3%, add to short portfolio (or exclude from any held long position) for 15 days.

**Key risk.** The gap itself is only observable after the open. Must use next-bar (next day's close) as entry, not the gap open.

---

### H183: Fundamental Confirmation of PEAD

**Hypothesis.** PEAD is stronger when the earnings surprise is accompanied by fundamental quality signals (low accruals, improving profitability). A stock that beats earnings via accounting adjustments (high accruals) should not exhibit the same post-announcement drift as a stock that beats via genuine cash flow improvement (low accruals). Filter H180's PEAD entries: only enter on positive surprises where the stock also has below-median accruals.

**Signal.** H180 signal with additional filter: accruals_to_assets < median of S&P 500 for that quarter. This reduces the number of entries by ~40–50% but should improve per-entry quality.

**Expected outcome.** Fewer trades, higher per-trade Sharpe. Watch CI carefully — reduced trade count may widen CI.

---

### H184: PEAD with Momentum Confirmation

**Hypothesis.** Combine PEAD with 3-month pre-announcement momentum: only enter the post-earnings long position if the stock's 3-month return *before* the announcement was positive (the stock was already in an uptrend). This filters out "dead cat bounce" situations where a deeply beaten-down stock finally beats low expectations.

**Signal.** H180 signal with additional filter: stock's 63-day return > 0 at announcement date. This ensures the post-announcement entry is in the direction of the existing trend.

**Combined model.** This signal is a precursor to the Batch 10 ML model — it combines a fundamental event (earnings surprise) with price momentum, a cross-signal interaction that a linear model captures naturally.

## BATCH 8: Liquidity-Aware Short-Term Reversal (H185–H189)
**Papers: Amihud (2002), Avellaneda & Lee (2010), Gatev et al. (2006)**

**Thesis.** Short-term reversal (Lehmann 1990, Lo-MacKinlay 1990) is a well-documented phenomenon: stocks that fall sharply in the prior week tend to bounce back the following week. The effect is driven by liquidity provision: market makers and dealers absorb one-sided order flow and earn a reversal as the temporary price impact reverts. The Amihud (2002) liquidity measure informs both the signal quality and the cost model — more liquid stocks have smaller reversals but lower costs; less liquid stocks have larger reversals but higher costs.

**H001–H100 application.** H040 (weekly reversal on SPY, Sharpe 0.481) nearly passed the keep bar. The lesson: the reversal mechanism is real but the signal-to-noise is weak on the liquid ETF. Cross-sectional reversal within individual stocks is more reliable because the reversal is driven by stock-specific liquidity shocks, not market-wide order flow.

**Cost warning.** This is the highest-cost batch. Weekly rebalancing of 100 stocks = ~5000 position changes per year. At 2.5 bps/turn, this is 125 bps/year cost drag. Pre-cost Sharpe needs to be > 0.90 to clear the keep bar after costs. These strategies are most viable as institutional HFT strategies — for this program, focus on testing the signal direction and adjust to see if monthly/biweekly versions survive costs.

---

### H185: 1-Week Reversal, Sector-Neutral

**Hypothesis.** Among S&P 500 stocks, the biggest losers in the prior 5 trading days tend to outperform the following 5 days. This is a liquidity-provision effect. Sector-neutralizing the signal removes macro rotations (sector sell-offs) and isolates the stock-specific component.

**Signal.** Prior-week return = return from close(t−5) to close(t−1). Sector-neutral rank descending (rank within sector, then standardize). Long the top quintile within each sector. Rebalance every 5 trading days (weekly).

**Expected pre-cost Sharpe.** 0.90–1.30 (reversal signals in liquid stocks are typically strong pre-cost). **Expected post-cost Sharpe.** 0.40–0.70 (costs severely erode the signal). This is the most cost-sensitive strategy in the program.

---

### H186: Residual Short-Term Reversal

**Hypothesis.** Remove market and sector returns from each stock's weekly return, then trade the reversal of the residual. The pure stock-specific reversal (after accounting for macro moves) should have a higher signal-to-noise ratio than the raw return reversal.

**Signal.** Regress each stock's weekly return on: (1) SPY weekly return (market component), (2) sector ETF weekly return (sector component). The residual is the stock-specific weekly move. Rank stocks by residual descending (stocks with large positive idiosyncratic moves will have future negative residual = reversal). Long bottom residual quintile. Rebalance weekly.

**Expected improvement over H185.** Residual reversal removes the sector momentum component (which doesn't revert), leaving only the stock-specific noise that does revert. Expected: higher Sharpe before costs, similar cost structure.

---

### H187: Liquidity-Filtered Reversal

**Hypothesis.** The reversal effect is strongest in the least liquid stocks (where temporary price pressure is largest). Among S&P 500 stocks (all reasonably liquid), the reversal effect is strongest in the smaller-cap, lower-ADV names. Filter to trade only the top-50% by Amihud illiquidity (higher reversal premium, but higher costs too).

**Signal.** Amihud illiquidity = mean(|daily_return| / dollar_volume) over trailing 21 days. Rank stocks by Amihud illiquidity descending. Apply H185 reversal signal only to the top half by illiquidity. This concentrates in the less liquid S&P 500 names.

**Cost adjustment.** Apply 3.5 bps/turn for the less liquid names (vs 2.5 bps for full universe). This strategy tests whether the higher reversal premium for illiquid names exceeds the higher cost of trading them.

---

### H188: Turnover-Constrained Monthly Reversal

**Hypothesis.** Monthly reversal (prior-month losers outperform next month) has lower turnover than weekly reversal but may still have a signal. At monthly frequency, the signal transitions from pure liquidity reversal to a regime that overlaps with short-term mean reversion. The advantage: 12–15 bps/year cost drag vs. 125 bps/year for weekly.

**Signal.** Prior-month return = return over the prior 21 trading days. Sector-neutral rank. Long bottom quintile (prior losers). Rebalance monthly. Exclude the most recent 5 days (revert-1 skip to avoid microstructure noise).

**Expected outcome.** Lower cost drag = higher probability of surviving the keep bar after costs. Pre-cost Sharpe likely lower than H185 (monthly signal is weaker), but post-cost Sharpe may be competitive. Target: post-cost Sharpe > 0.52.

---

### H189: Residual Momentum after Sector-Factor Regression (Avellaneda-Lee)

**Hypothesis.** Avellaneda & Lee use factor models (PCA or ETF regression) to isolate residual returns. Stocks with extreme negative residual z-scores (relative to their historical distribution) tend to mean-revert. This is a hybrid of momentum (the signal uses a stock's current z-score relative to its own history) and mean reversion (extreme z-scores tend to revert).

**Signal.** For each stock, compute the rolling 252-day mean and standard deviation of its factor-residual return (residual after regressing on SPY + sector ETF). Current residual z-score = (today's residual − trailing_252d_mean) / trailing_252d_std. Long stocks with z-score < −2.0 (deeply below their own history). Rebalance daily or weekly.

**Implementation constraint.** Fitting factor regressions rolling over 252 days for 500 stocks is computationally intensive. Vectorize with matrix operations; do not loop over stocks individually.

## BATCH 9: Relative Value, Pairs, and PCA Statistical Arbitrage (H190–H194)
**Papers: Gatev, Goetzmann & Rouwenhorst (2006), Avellaneda & Lee (2010)**

**Thesis.** Pairs trading exploits price divergences between historically similar stocks, betting on convergence. PCA-based statistical arbitrage models stock returns as driven by latent common factors and trades the mean reversion of residuals. Both strategies produce near-zero market exposure (they are long one stock and short another), providing true market-neutral returns.

**H001–H100 application.** No equivalent strategies were ever tested — this batch is entirely new territory. H001 corr for a well-constructed pairs trade should approach 0.0 by construction (the portfolio is long and short within the same sector). Expected H001 corr: +0.05 to +0.25.

**Critical cost concern.** Pairs trading requires shorting, which adds borrow costs. Within-sector pairs trades are typically available to borrow (the short legs are large-cap liquid names), but costs must be accounted for. Model borrow at 50 bps/year on the gross short exposure.

---

### H190: Classic Distance Pairs (Gatev et al.)

**Hypothesis.** Find pairs of S&P 500 stocks with historically similar price paths (minimum normalized price distance during a 12-month formation window). When their price spread diverges by > 2 standard deviations from the formation-period mean, buy the underperformer and short the outperformer. Close when the spread reverts to 0 or after 6 months, whichever comes first.

**Signal.** Formation period: first 12 months of each train window. Compute normalized price series (divide each stock's price by its formation-period mean). Find the 20 pairs with minimum sum-of-squared-differences. Trading period: monitor all 20 pairs. Open when spread > 2σ, close at 0 or after 6 months. Require both stocks to be in the S&P 500 at signal time.

**H001–H100 application.** The pairs signal is by construction market-neutral — long and short positions in each pair cancel SPY beta. Expected H001 corr: +0.05 to +0.15. This is potentially the lowest-H001-corr strategy in H150–H200.

**Key risk (from paper).** Pairs break during regime shifts (sector M&A, bankruptcy, competitive disruption). The 6-month stop-loss is the primary protection. Also: pairs trading returns have been declining since the early 2000s as the trade has become more crowded.

---

### H191: Sector-Constrained Pairs Trading

**Hypothesis.** Improve H190 by constraining pairs to be within the same GICS sector. Sector-neutral pairs have lower breakage risk (both stocks face the same sector-level macro conditions) and more predictable convergence behavior. Allow up to 50 active pairs (5 per sector, 10 GICS sectors).

**Signal.** Same as H190 but formation matches are only made within each sector. The 5 tightest pairs per sector are monitored each trading period. This prevents cross-sector pairs (e.g., oil company paired with tech stock) that are mechanically similar in one bull market but uncorrelated in others.

**Expected improvement over H190.** Lower breakage rate, more stable fold-by-fold performance. Slightly higher transaction costs (sector constraint reduces the cheapest pairs to trade).

---

### H192: Cointegration Pairs (Engle-Granger)

**Hypothesis.** Instead of price-distance matching, use Engle-Granger cointegration testing to identify pairs whose price spread is stationary (mean-reverting). Cointegrated pairs have a more stable economic relationship than distance pairs — the spread is guaranteed to be stationary under the null hypothesis, not just empirically similar.

**Signal.** Formation: fit OLS regression of stock A price on stock B price. Test the residuals for stationarity (ADF test, p < 0.05). For cointegrated pairs, the spread is the regression residual. Trade when z-score of spread > 2.0 or < −2.0. Exit at z-score = 0 or at 3-month time stop.

**Key implementation risk.** Testing cointegration on a training sample and applying it OOS is valid, but the cointegration relationship may not hold in the test period. Use robust stopping criteria (close position if loss exceeds 1.5 σ of formation-period spread) to limit false-convergence losses.

### H193: Sector ETF Residual Mean Reversion (Avellaneda-Lee Method 1)

**Hypothesis.** Regress each stock's daily return on: (1) SPY return and (2) its sector ETF return. The residual is the stock's idiosyncratic return after removing market and sector effects. Over time, each stock's residual follows a mean-reverting process. When a stock's residual z-score falls below −2.0 (idiosyncratic crash), it tends to revert; when it rises above +2.0, it tends to fall back.

**Signal.** For each stock, fit rolling OLS over the past 252 days: r_stock = α + β₁×r_SPY + β₂×r_sector + ε. Compute the cumulative residual (OU process proxy). Estimate the mean-reversion speed. Open long when z-score < −2.0; open short when z-score > +2.0. Close when z-score crosses 0 or at 30-day time stop.

**This is the most theoretically grounded short-term strategy in the batch.** It requires clean factor model fitting with strict lookahead avoidance.

---

### H194: PCA Residual Mean Reversion (Avellaneda-Lee Method 2)

**Hypothesis.** Replace the ETF factor model with PCA. Fit PCA on rolling 252-day return matrix of all S&P 500 stocks. Extract the top 15 principal components as common factors. The residual of each stock after projecting onto these 15 PCs is the idiosyncratic component. Trade mean reversion of this idiosyncratic component with the same z-score framework as H193.

**Key risk.** PCA on a rolling window suffers from look-ahead bias if not implemented correctly. The PCA must be fit only on data available before the test window starts, and re-fit at the start of each new train period. The components themselves are not economically interpretable — they are latent market factors.

**Expected H001 corr.** +0.05 to +0.20. The PCA model removes essentially all market and sector beta by construction. True idiosyncratic mean reversion is near-zero corr with SPY.

## BATCH 10: Machine Learning Meta-Model and Cross-Program Ensemble (H195–H200)
**Papers: Gu, Kelly & Xiu (2020), Asness, Moskowitz & Pedersen (2013), Fama & French (2015)**

**Thesis.** After implementing all interpretable factor and event strategies (H150–H194), the ML meta-model is justified only if it demonstrably outperforms the best interpretable factor composite (H173, FF5 composite). The Gu-Kelly-Xiu (2020) result showed ML models improve on simple linear models for 1-month return prediction — but this was in a broad universe with thousands of stocks. In the S&P 500 (500 stocks), the regularization problem is more severe and the improvement may be smaller. The final strategy (H200) is the cross-program MDR ensemble combining the best strategies from both H001–H100 and H150–H199.

**H001–H100 application.** H095 (MaxDiversificationPortfolio, MDR ensemble, Sharpe 1.133) is the terminal H001–H100 result. The cross-program ensemble at H200 will combine H095's asset-allocation strategies with H150–H199's cross-sectional strategies. These two program types are structurally complementary: asset allocation (H001–H100) decides market-level exposure; cross-sectional (H150–H200) decides stock selection. Together they form a complete portfolio management architecture.

### H195: Regularized Linear Factor Model (Elastic Net Baseline)

**Hypothesis.** Before using nonlinear ML, establish the best possible linear model. Elastic net regression (L1 + L2 regularization) on a feature matrix of all paper-derived signals predicts each stock's next-month return rank. This is the baseline against which H196 (gradient boosting) must improve.

**Signal.** Feature matrix (computed using only lagged data): 12-1 momentum, 52w-high ratio, smooth momentum (fraction positive days), value composite (B/M + E/P), quality composite (gross profitability + accruals), defensive composite (beta + idiosyncratic vol), asset growth, accruals, book-to-market, earnings yield, EPS surprise (if available). Elastic net cross-validation on the training fold to select regularization strength. Predict monthly return rank. Long top quintile of predictions.

**Comparison.** The H173 FF5 composite is essentially an equal-weight version of this model (equal z-score combination). Elastic net allows the model to weight features optimally. If the elastic net is no better than equal weighting (H173), it means the signal weights are genuinely not estimable from the training data alone — the features are too noisy to distinguish optimal weights from noise.

### H196: Gradient-Boosted Rank Model (Gu, Kelly & Xiu 2020)

**Hypothesis.** A gradient-boosted decision tree (LightGBM or XGBoost) using paper-derived features can capture nonlinear interactions between factors that the linear model misses. For example: the combination of high momentum AND low accruals is worth more than the sum of each factor's individual contribution (synergy of clean earnings trend). Tree models capture these interactions naturally.

**Signal.** Same feature matrix as H195. Target: next-month return rank (binned into 5 equal groups). Fit gradient boosting with walk-forward expanding window. Predict quintile for each stock. Long top quintile predictions.

**Overfitting safeguards (mandatory).**
- Use only features derived from papers H001–H020 above. No fishing expeditions.
- Hyperparameters (max_depth, n_estimators, learning_rate, min_child_weight) must be cross-validated on the training fold only.
- Report OOS feature importance (SHAP values) to verify model is not learning noise.
- Compare in-sample vs OOS Sharpe: if OOS < 70% of in-sample, the model is overfitting.
- Required monotonic sanity check: do predicted top-quintile stocks actually have on average higher realized returns than predicted bottom-quintile?

---

### H197: ML Ensemble of Interpretable Models (Random Forest of Factor Sub-Models)

**Hypothesis.** Instead of one big ML model, fit separate ML models for each factor family (momentum, value, quality, event) and ensemble their predictions. Each sub-model uses only the features from its paper-family. The ensemble of sub-models is less prone to overfitting than a single large model because each sub-model is well-identified within its domain.

**Signal.** Four sub-models:
1. Momentum sub-model: features = 12-1 momentum, 52w-high, fraction positive days, prior-month return
2. Value sub-model: features = B/M, E/P, FCF yield, asset growth
3. Quality sub-model: features = gross profitability, operating profitability, accruals, leverage
4. Event sub-model: features = EPS surprise, revenue surprise, announcement gap (if available)

Equal-weight blend of the four sub-model predictions. Long top quintile of the blended score.

**Comparison.** H196 (single large model) vs H197 (ensemble of sub-models) directly tests whether feature interaction across factor domains (e.g., "high momentum AND high quality") adds value beyond domain-specific models.

---

### H198: Momentum + Quality ML Filter (Cross-Factor Interaction Focus)

**Hypothesis.** The most documented cross-factor interaction in the literature is momentum + quality (or momentum + accruals). Stocks with high momentum AND high earnings quality have stronger drift than pure momentum stocks (because their past returns are more likely to reflect genuine fundamental improvement rather than speculation). Train an ML model specifically to learn this interaction.

**Signal.** Feature matrix focused on cross-factor interactions: 12-1 momentum × gross_profitability, 12-1 momentum × (−accruals), 12-1 momentum × F-score, value_composite × momentum, quality_composite × momentum. All interactions computed as z-score products. Gradient boosting on these interaction features. Long top quintile.

**Purpose.** This targeted model tests whether the specific interaction terms matter, or whether the interactions are captured incidentally by the full model (H196).

### H199: Meta-Allocator (Walk-Forward Strategy Sleeve Weighting)

**Hypothesis.** Rather than building a new stock-selection signal, H199 allocates dynamically between the already-built strategy sleeves (H150–H198 kept strategies) using ML. In each 2-year test period, the meta-allocator is trained on the prior 5 years of daily returns from each kept strategy sleeve. It predicts which strategies will outperform in the next month and overweights them.

**Signal.** Feature matrix: trailing 1m, 3m, 6m, 12m Sharpe of each kept H150-H198 strategy. Gradient boosting regression predicts next-month return of each strategy. Weights = proportional to positive predictions; equal weight when all predictions negative.

**Lesson from H001–H100 ensembles.** H092 (regime-adaptive weighting of H001–H100 strategies) barely beat equal-weight. Anti-momentum rebalancing (H093) destroyed value. Constraint: never reduce any strategy's weight below 50% of its equal-weight allocation — this prevents the meta-allocator from piling out of strategies during normal drawdowns. All component strategies are S&P 500 stock portfolios from H150–H198.

### H200: S&P 500 Cross-Sectional MDR Ensemble (Terminal Strategy)

**Hypothesis.** The terminal strategy: run the MaxDiversificationRatio (MDR) optimizer across all kept H150–H199 strategies. The MDR portfolio maximizes the ratio of weighted-average component volatility to portfolio volatility — maximizing genuine diversification within the S&P 500 stock-selection program. Each component strategy produces a daily return series from its stock portfolio; MDR finds the combination that minimizes correlation drag.

**Construction.**
- Pool all kept strategies from H150–H199 (estimated 15–25 strategies).
- Apply MDR optimization (SLSQP) with constraints: no strategy exceeds 25% weight; all weights ≥ 0.
- All component strategies use the same cross-sectional panel engine and the same point-in-time S&P 500 membership — no external assets, no ETF exposure.
- Combine at the daily portfolio return level (each strategy's daily PnL weighted by MDR allocation).

**Why the MDR ensemble works here.** Long-only momentum (H150), market-neutral momentum (H151), sector-neutral momentum (H153), value-momentum composite (H162), quality (H165–H169), defensive low-beta (H175), and reversal (H185–H188) all capture different slices of the S&P 500 return cross-section with imperfect pairwise correlations. MDR finds the allocation that minimizes redundancy — the same mechanism that made H095 (Sharpe 1.133) the H001–H100 champion.

**Expected outcome.**
- Sharpe: 1.20–1.50 (diversification across factor families within S&P 500 stocks)
- Max DD: < −15% (market-neutral components dampen drawdowns)
- Component correlation range: +0.10 to +0.70 (momentum strategies correlated; reversal and neutral strategies diversify)

**This is purely an S&P 500 stock portfolio.** All positions are individual S&P 500 constituent stocks. No ETFs, no bonds, no cross-program mixing.
