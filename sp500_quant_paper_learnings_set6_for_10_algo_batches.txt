S&P 500 QUANT TRADING PAPER LEARNINGS - SET 6
For planning 10 batches of trading algorithms with Claude Code CLI

Purpose:
This file summarizes another non-overlapping set of 20 quant trading, accounting,
corporate finance, market microstructure, and financial economics papers. The
goal is not to turn every paper into a finished strategy immediately. The goal is
to give a chatbot enough structured context to plan 10 batches of S&P 500
algorithm development with fewer wasted tokens, clearer feature specs, and faster
iteration.

Set 6 theme:
Corporate events, earnings expectations, SEC filing text, conference-call tone,
product-market competition, policy exposure, innovation and patents, credit risk,
ETF/passive ownership, and institutional ownership horizons.

How this set differs from the prior five:
- Prior sets focused heavily on classic anomalies, factor models, volatility,
  macro prediction, portfolio construction, machine learning, governance, ESG,
  sentiment, and intraday liquidity.
- Set 6 is more "event plus information architecture." It teaches you how to
  detect when the S&P 500 market is slow to process firm-specific information.
- Many signals here are lower frequency, but they can be combined with faster
  price/volume execution logic.
- Several papers require non-price data. Build them as feature modules first,
  then attach trading rules only after point-in-time feature validation.

Important guardrails:
- This is research infrastructure, not financial advice.
- Most papers use broader U.S. equity universes. For the S&P 500, signal strength
  may be weaker because large-cap names are more followed and more efficiently
  priced.
- Use point-in-time S&P 500 membership, point-in-time accounting data, and
  delisting-aware returns whenever possible.
- Do not trust a signal until it survives transaction costs, slippage, realistic
  announcement timestamps, sector neutrality tests, and walk-forward validation.
- Avoid shorting hard-to-borrow or high-event-risk names without explicit borrow,
  liquidity, and gap-risk modeling.

THE 20 PAPERS

PAPER 01: Bernard and Thomas (1989) - Post-Earnings-Announcement Drift

Core idea:
Post-earnings-announcement drift, or PEAD, shows that stocks with positive
earnings surprises often continue to outperform after the announcement, while
negative-surprise stocks often continue to underperform. The paper is useful
because it frames drift as delayed price response rather than only compensation
for risk.

Trading lesson:
The market may underreact to standardized unexpected earnings, especially when
the earnings information is complex or when investors anchor on old expectations.

S&P 500 implementation:
- Build a standardized unexpected earnings score using actual EPS minus
  consensus EPS, scaled by historical surprise volatility or price.
- Run an event-time strategy after earnings announcements, not before.
- Delay entry until timestamps are clean. If the announcement is after market
  close, earliest clean entry is next open or next close, depending on your
  execution model.
- Hold for 20 to 60 trading days, then test shorter and longer variants.

Features:
- EPS surprise percentile
- revenue surprise percentile
- gross-margin surprise
- guidance revision after announcement
- analyst estimate revision after announcement
- announcement timing: before open, after close, intraday

Failure modes:
- Lookahead from incorrect earnings timestamps
- Using final analyst consensus instead of pre-announcement consensus
- Treating all surprises equally without sector or volatility controls
- Ignoring that large-cap S&P 500 names are heavily watched


PAPER 02: Skinner and Sloan (2002) - Earnings Surprises, Growth Expectations, and Stock Returns

Core idea:
High-growth stocks can be punished severely when they miss earnings expectations.
The market embeds optimistic expectations into growth firms, and negative
surprises can trigger sharp repricing.

Trading lesson:
Earnings surprise signals should be conditioned on valuation and embedded growth
expectations. A negative surprise in an expensive growth stock is not the same as
a negative surprise in a cheap low-expectation stock.

S&P 500 implementation:
- Create a "torpedo risk" score before earnings:
  high valuation + high expected growth + high analyst optimism + high recent
  multiple expansion.
- After earnings, short or underweight high-torpedo-risk firms with negative EPS
  or guidance surprises.
- For long-only portfolios, use the score as a risk-reduction filter before
  earnings.

Features:
- forward P/E percentile
- price-to-sales percentile
- sales growth expectation
- EPS growth expectation
- analyst optimism vs trailing fundamentals
- earnings surprise sign and magnitude

Failure modes:
- Shorting high-quality compounders solely because they are expensive
- Failing to separate expected high growth from actual delivered high growth
- Not controlling for sector valuation norms


PAPER 03: So (2013) - Predicting Analyst Forecast Errors

Core idea:
Investors may overweight analyst forecasts. If analyst forecast errors can be
predicted, then returns can be forecast because prices partially inherit the
same analyst optimism or pessimism.

Trading lesson:
Do not use analyst forecasts only as a clean expectation benchmark. Forecasts can
themselves be biased signals.

S&P 500 implementation:
- Build a forecast-error prediction model:
  predicted error = function of forecast revisions, dispersion, past forecast
  bias, accruals, profitability, valuation, and recent returns.
- Long firms where analysts appear too pessimistic.
- Short or underweight firms where analysts appear too optimistic.
- Combine with earnings announcement windows to capture correction events.

Features:
- consensus EPS forecast level
- 1-month and 3-month forecast revision
- analyst dispersion
- analyst count
- historical forecast bias by firm and analyst group
- predicted forecast error

Failure modes:
- IBES/forecast data timestamp errors
- Forecasts issued after price-moving news but treated as prior expectations
- Overfitting analyst-bias models in small sector samples


PAPER 04: Li (2010) - Forward-Looking Statements in Corporate Filings

Core idea:
Forward-looking statements in MD&A sections of 10-K and 10-Q filings contain
information about future performance. Simple machine-learning text classification
can extract useful signal from management's forward-looking language.

Trading lesson:
SEC filings are not just compliance documents. Management's future-oriented
sentences can become structured features.

S&P 500 implementation:
- Parse 10-K and 10-Q MD&A sections.
- Identify forward-looking sentences using keyword rules first, then a supervised
  classifier if labels are available.
- Score positive, negative, uncertain, and modal language in forward-looking
  sentences.
- Trade only after SEC filing acceptance timestamps, not fiscal period end dates.

Features:
- forward-looking sentence count
- positive FLS ratio
- negative FLS ratio
- uncertainty ratio
- change in FLS tone vs prior filing
- sector-relative FLS score

Failure modes:
- Parsing wrong filing sections
- Using filing dates rather than SEC acceptance timestamps
- Treating boilerplate forward-looking disclaimers as management information
- Ignoring changes in disclosure rules and firm templates


PAPER 05: Li (2008) - Annual Report Readability, Current Earnings, and Earnings Persistence

Core idea:
Annual report readability is related to firm performance and earnings
persistence. More complex or less readable reports can indicate less transparent
or less persistent earnings.

Trading lesson:
Text complexity can be a risk feature. Complicated disclosure may degrade the
quality of earnings signals or indicate hidden operating issues.

S&P 500 implementation:
- Compute readability and complexity metrics for 10-K filings.
- Combine readability with earnings quality:
  strong earnings + readable filing = higher-quality signal;
  strong earnings + unreadable filing = lower-confidence signal.
- Use complexity as a risk penalty in earnings, quality, and value strategies.

Features:
- filing length
- average sentence length
- word complexity
- financial jargon density
- boilerplate similarity to prior filing
- readability z-score by firm and sector

Failure modes:
- Penalizing naturally complex firms such as banks, insurers, and industrial
  conglomerates without sector controls
- Confusing longer disclosure with worse disclosure
- Ignoring changes from prior-year readability


PAPER 06: Mayew and Venkatachalam (2012) - The Power of Voice

Core idea:
Managerial vocal affect during conference calls contains information about firm
prospects and is associated with market reactions and future performance.

Trading lesson:
How management says something can contain incremental signal beyond the transcript
text. Voice stress, emotion, uncertainty, and confidence may help identify
whether the market is underreacting to call information.

S&P 500 implementation:
- Start with transcript-only proxies because audio pipelines are slower.
- Then add optional audio features for firms where recordings are available.
- Score CEO/CFO prepared remarks separately from Q&A.
- Look for divergence:
  positive transcript tone but negative vocal affect, or vice versa.

Features:
- transcript tone
- Q&A uncertainty
- answer evasiveness
- prepared remarks vs Q&A sentiment gap
- audio pitch variability
- speech rate
- vocal stress/affect score

Failure modes:
- Poor audio quality
- Speaker diarization errors
- Overfitting voice features with small samples
- Ignoring that call participants, formats, and microphones vary by firm


PAPER 07: Hoberg and Phillips (2016) - Text-Based Network Industries and Endogenous Product Differentiation

Core idea:
10-K product descriptions can be used to build dynamic, text-based industry
networks. These networks identify competitors more flexibly than static SIC or
GICS classifications.

Trading lesson:
Peer relationships are dynamic. Product-similarity networks can detect economic
exposures and rival shocks that standard sector labels miss.

S&P 500 implementation:
- Use Hoberg-Phillips TNIC-style similarity data if available, or approximate it
  from product descriptions in 10-K filings.
- Build peer baskets for each S&P 500 firm based on product text similarity.
- Use peer earnings surprises, guidance changes, and return shocks as predictors
  for firms that have not yet reported.

Features:
- text-based peer set
- peer earnings surprise
- peer revenue surprise
- peer return shock
- peer guidance revision
- own-firm product differentiation score

Failure modes:
- Static peer lists that ignore product evolution
- Overlapping peers causing crowded signals
- Not separating supply-chain links from product-market competition


PAPER 08: Hoberg, Phillips, and Prabhala (2014) - Product Market Threats, Payouts, and Financial Flexibility

Core idea:
Product market threats affect corporate payout policy and cash holdings. Product
market fluidity captures how rivals' product descriptions move toward a firm's
product space.

Trading lesson:
Competitive threat can become a firm-level risk state. When rivals move toward a
firm's product market, that firm may need cash flexibility and may face weaker
payout or margin prospects.

S&P 500 implementation:
- Use product-market fluidity as a slow-moving risk feature.
- Penalize firms with high fluidity, declining margins, and declining buybacks.
- Look for defensive cash buildup or payout reductions as confirmation that
  management sees competitive pressure.

Features:
- product market fluidity
- rival similarity increase
- cash-to-assets change
- payout reduction
- repurchase reduction
- gross-margin compression

Failure modes:
- Treating all competitive threat as bearish; some firms respond with successful
  innovation
- Ignoring industry life cycle
- Using product text after the market already reacted to the filing


PAPER 09: Cohen, Diether, and Malloy (2013) - Legislating Stock Prices

Core idea:
Legislators' votes can reveal information about how legislation will affect firms
in their constituencies. The market may not fully process the implications of
complex legislation immediately.

Trading lesson:
Policy events can be transformed into firm-level signals when you map legislation
to affected industries, geographies, and firms.

S&P 500 implementation:
- Build a policy-event graph:
  bill -> legislators -> constituent industries/firms -> expected beneficiary or
  loser.
- For S&P 500, start with major regulation-heavy sectors:
  healthcare, defense, energy, utilities, telecom, financials, and industrials.
- Treat the first version as an event study, not an always-on strategy.

Features:
- bill passage event timestamp
- sector/industry exposure
- state/district revenue or employment exposure
- legislator interest score
- vote direction
- bill complexity proxy

Failure modes:
- Incorrect firm-to-legislation mapping
- Public-news leakage before formal vote
- Partisan or narrative bias in manual labels
- Low event count for robust backtesting


PAPER 10: Hirshleifer, Hsu, and Li (2013) - Innovative Efficiency and Stock Returns

Core idea:
Innovation efficiency, measured as innovation output relative to R&D input, is
associated with future operating performance and stock returns.

Trading lesson:
Do not look only at R&D spending. Look at how much useful innovation a firm gets
per dollar of R&D.

S&P 500 implementation:
- Build innovative efficiency:
  patents or citations divided by R&D capital or R&D expense.
- Rank firms within sectors because patent intensity differs dramatically across
  industries.
- Combine with valuation to avoid overpaying for obvious innovation leaders.

Features:
- patents per R&D dollar
- citations per R&D dollar
- patent growth
- citation growth
- R&D capital
- sector-relative innovation efficiency

Failure modes:
- Patent grant lags
- Sector differences in patenting behavior
- Patent quantity without quality
- Treating accounting R&D as the only innovation input


PAPER 11: Kogan, Papanikolaou, Seru, and Stoffman (2017) - Technological Innovation, Resource Allocation, and Growth

Core idea:
Patent data can be used to estimate the economic value of innovation. Valuable
innovation affects firm growth, reallocation, and market value.

Trading lesson:
Patent value matters more than patent count. The market impact of innovation is
strongest when the innovation is economically important, not merely numerous.

S&P 500 implementation:
- If patent-value data is available, use it directly.
- Otherwise approximate patent value using citations, patent family size,
  originality, generality, and market reaction around patent grant dates.
- Use patent-value shocks as medium-horizon features.

Features:
- patent-value estimate
- citation-weighted patent count
- patent originality
- patent generality
- technology-class momentum
- firm patent shock vs history

Failure modes:
- Grant-date lag and publication-date confusion
- Survivorship in patent databases
- Overweighting technology sectors
- Ignoring acquisitions of patent portfolios


PAPER 12: Eberhart, Maxwell, and Siddique (2004) - R&D Increases and Long-Term Abnormal Returns

Core idea:
Firms that unexpectedly increase R&D expenditures experience positive long-term
abnormal returns and operating performance. The market may underreact to the
benefits of R&D investment.

Trading lesson:
Unexpected R&D increases can be positive when they reflect productive investment,
not when they reflect desperate spending.

S&P 500 implementation:
- Identify unexpected R&D increases relative to firm history and industry peers.
- Confirm with balance-sheet strength, margin stability, and patent output.
- Use a 6- to 24-month horizon sleeve rather than a short event trade.

Features:
- R&D expense growth
- R&D-to-sales change
- R&D surprise vs historical model
- cash balance
- patent follow-through
- revenue growth follow-through

Failure modes:
- R&D capitalization/accounting differences
- Firms that acquire innovation rather than produce it internally
- Treating all R&D increases as good without balance-sheet and profitability
  filters


PAPER 13: Merton (1974) - On the Pricing of Corporate Debt

Core idea:
Corporate debt can be modeled through the option-like structure of firm assets.
Equity resembles a call option on firm value after debt is paid. This framework
leads to structural default-risk measures such as distance to default.

Trading lesson:
Equity risk changes nonlinearly as leverage and asset volatility change. Credit
stress can be translated into equity trading features.

S&P 500 implementation:
- Estimate a simplified distance-to-default signal using market equity, debt,
  equity volatility, and a liability threshold.
- Use changes in distance to default, not only the level.
- Combine with credit spreads and rating watch events when available.

Features:
- market leverage
- equity volatility
- distance to default
- change in distance to default
- debt maturity proxy
- credit spread proxy

Failure modes:
- Noisy asset-value estimation
- Accounting debt updates only quarterly
- Model assumptions can break during crisis regimes
- Distress names can rebound violently


PAPER 14: Vassalou and Xing (2004) - Default Risk in Equity Returns

Core idea:
Default-risk measures derived from Merton-style models can be linked to equity
returns. The paper helps connect credit risk and stock returns.

Trading lesson:
Default risk is not a simple "high risk equals high return" feature. It must be
handled with regime, size, value, and liquidity controls.

S&P 500 implementation:
- Compute default-likelihood proxies for all S&P 500 constituents.
- Use default-risk changes as a warning overlay for long strategies.
- Test whether distressed momentum/reversal differs in high-default-risk names.

Features:
- default likelihood
- distance-to-default percentile
- default-risk change
- CDS spread change when available
- credit spread beta
- high-yield spread regime

Failure modes:
- Low number of true distress firms inside S&P 500 in normal markets
- Distress rallies after policy interventions
- Ignoring sector-specific balance-sheet norms


PAPER 15: Avramov, Chordia, Jostova, and Philipov (2007) - Momentum and Credit Rating

Core idea:
Momentum profits are strongly related to credit rating. Momentum can be large
among low-grade firms and weak or absent among high-grade firms.

Trading lesson:
Momentum should be conditioned on credit quality. A price trend in a financially
fragile company is different from a trend in a high-quality company.

S&P 500 implementation:
- Segment momentum strategies by credit rating buckets:
  AAA/AA, A, BBB, below investment grade or unrated.
- For S&P 500, focus on BBB and downgrade-watch names because the index has fewer
  deep junk firms.
- Add crash-risk controls for low-grade momentum.

Features:
- 12-1 momentum
- 6-1 momentum
- credit rating bucket
- rating outlook/watch
- downgrade event
- credit spread change

Failure modes:
- Small sample of low-grade S&P 500 names
- Momentum crash exposure
- Rating changes lag market information
- Credit ratings are discrete and stale


PAPER 16: Kisgen (2006) - Credit Ratings and Capital Structure

Core idea:
Credit ratings affect corporate capital-structure decisions. Firms near a rating
change may adjust debt issuance, equity issuance, or leverage to protect or
improve ratings.

Trading lesson:
Rating thresholds can create predictable corporate behavior. Firms near rating
boundaries may manage leverage more aggressively.

S&P 500 implementation:
- Identify firms near rating upgrade or downgrade thresholds.
- Track net debt issuance, buybacks, dividends, and cash retention.
- Combine with rating outlook and credit-spread movement to detect likely
  de-risking or rating pressure.

Features:
- rating notch distance
- net debt issuance
- leverage change
- payout change
- cash accumulation
- rating outlook

Failure modes:
- Limited public rating granularity
- Corporate behavior may be driven by management strategy rather than rating
  targeting
- Rating agencies lag changes in market-implied credit quality


PAPER 17: Ben-David, Franzoni, and Moussawi (2018) - Do ETFs Increase Volatility?

Core idea:
ETF ownership can increase volatility and turnover in underlying securities. ETF
arbitrage and flows can transmit non-fundamental trading shocks into component
stocks.

Trading lesson:
Passive ownership is not neutral for short-horizon trading. ETF ownership can
alter volatility, turnover, and liquidity risk.

S&P 500 implementation:
- Measure ETF ownership and ETF flow exposure for each S&P 500 stock.
- Use ETF-flow shocks as a volatility and mean-reversion state variable.
- For SPY/IVV/VOO-heavy names, monitor index-level flow and futures pressure.

Features:
- ETF ownership percentage
- ETF ownership change
- ETF flow exposure
- intraday volatility
- turnover shock
- index-flow beta

Failure modes:
- ETF ownership is widespread in S&P 500; cross-sectional dispersion may be low
- ETF flow data can be delayed or expensive
- Confusing index beta with ETF-specific flow pressure


PAPER 18: Israeli, Lee, and Sridharan (2017) - Is There a Dark Side to Exchange Traded Funds?

Core idea:
Higher ETF ownership is associated with weaker information environment measures,
including higher trading costs, higher return synchronicity, lower future
earnings response coefficients, and lower analyst coverage.

Trading lesson:
ETF ownership can make prices move more with the basket and less with firm-
specific fundamentals. This can affect both signal strength and risk.

S&P 500 implementation:
- Treat ETF ownership as a crowding and information-environment feature.
- Downweight firm-specific signals when ETF ownership is high and return
  synchronicity is rising.
- Alternatively, look for delayed firm-specific reactions after ETF-driven moves.

Features:
- ETF ownership
- ETF ownership change
- stock return synchronicity
- analyst coverage change
- spread/liquidity change
- firm-specific return variance

Failure modes:
- Causality is difficult; ETFs may hold already liquid, index-like names
- S&P 500 names are inherently index-sensitive
- Analyst coverage may remain high for mega-caps despite ETF ownership


PAPER 19: Bushee (1998) - The Influence of Institutional Investors on Myopic R&D Investment Behavior

Core idea:
Institutional investor type matters. Transient institutional investors can
pressure managers toward short-term behavior, while dedicated institutions may
support long-term investment.

Trading lesson:
Institutional ownership is not one feature. Investor horizon and style can affect
corporate decisions and the quality of long-term investment.

S&P 500 implementation:
- Classify institutional owners into transient, quasi-indexer, and dedicated
  groups if possible.
- Use ownership-horizon mix to adjust confidence in R&D and long-horizon
  innovation signals.
- Watch for firms with high transient ownership cutting R&D to meet earnings
  targets.

Features:
- transient institutional ownership
- dedicated institutional ownership
- quasi-indexer ownership
- change in owner horizon mix
- R&D cut indicator
- earnings pressure indicator

Failure modes:
- 13F data is delayed
- Institution classification is nontrivial
- Ownership changes can reflect index flows rather than active information


PAPER 20: Yan and Zhang (2009) - Institutional Investors and Equity Returns

Core idea:
The positive relation between institutional ownership and future stock returns is
driven by short-term institutions. Short-term institutional trading can forecast
future returns and future earnings surprises.

Trading lesson:
Some short-horizon institutional trading may be informed. The ownership signal is
stronger when you distinguish short-term institutions from long-term holders.

S&P 500 implementation:
- Use quarterly 13F changes to infer ownership flows by institution type.
- Create a short-term institutional accumulation signal.
- Combine with upcoming earnings to test whether accumulation predicts positive
  surprises.

Features:
- short-term institution ownership change
- long-term institution ownership change
- net institutional accumulation
- institutional concentration
- earnings surprise after accumulation
- ownership-flow acceleration

Failure modes:
- 13F publication delay
- Quarterly frequency may be too slow for many strategies
- Hidden derivatives and non-13F positions
- Crowded institutional trades can reverse quickly

BATCH 01: Earnings Surprise and Analyst Expectation Error Engine

Papers used:
- PAPER 01: Bernard and Thomas
- PAPER 02: Skinner and Sloan
- PAPER 03: So

Strategy hypothesis:
The S&P 500 market still underreacts to earnings information when earnings
surprises interact with analyst forecast bias, valuation expectations, and
guidance revisions.

Data needed:
- point-in-time S&P 500 constituents
- earnings announcement timestamps
- actual EPS and revenue
- pre-announcement consensus EPS and revenue
- analyst revisions and dispersion
- price, volume, market cap, sector
- valuation metrics

Feature build:
1. Build standardized EPS surprise.
2. Build standardized revenue surprise.
3. Build analyst optimism/pessimism score.
4. Build pre-earnings torpedo-risk score.
5. Build post-earnings revision-confirmation score.

Signal versions:
- v1: long top SUE quintile, short bottom SUE quintile after announcement
- v2: v1 conditioned on analyst forecast-error prediction
- v3: short expensive high-growth firms after negative surprise
- v4: long positive surprise only when analyst revisions confirm within 5 days

Backtest rules:
- enter next open or next close after confirmed announcement timestamp
- hold 20, 40, and 60 trading days
- sector-neutral and beta-neutral variants
- exclude earnings announced during regular hours unless intraday timestamp is
  reliable

Expected failure tests:
- remove microcaps is irrelevant, but still test liquidity tiers inside S&P 500
- compare pre-2005, 2005-2015, and 2016-present
- test whether results survive mega-cap-only subset
- test long-only use as an earnings-risk overlay

Papers used:
- PAPER 04: Li (2010)
- PAPER 05: Li (2008)

Forward-looking filing language and readability changes contain information about

- v4: event trade after 10-Q/10-K acceptance timestamp

Backtest rules:
- enter after filing acceptance timestamp plus realistic processing delay
- hold 1, 3, and 6 months
- sector-neutral ranking
- test only firms with clean parsed MD&A

Expected failure tests:
- filings released after market close vs during market hours
- boilerplate filtering on/off
- financial sector excluded/included
- raw readability vs change in readability

BATCH 03: Earnings Call Tone, Q&A, and Voice Proxy Engine

Papers used:
- PAPER 06: Mayew and Venkatachalam

Strategy hypothesis:
Management tone, Q&A uncertainty, and vocal affect contain incremental
information around earnings calls. The market may underreact to subtle negative
or positive cues when headline numbers are mixed.

Data needed:
- earnings call transcripts
- optional call audio
- speaker labels
- earnings announcement timestamps
- price and volume data
- analyst questions and management answers

Feature build:
1. Separate prepared remarks from Q&A.
2. Separate CEO, CFO, and other speakers.
3. Score sentiment, uncertainty, and evasiveness.
4. Build prepared-vs-Q&A tone gap.
5. Optional: extract audio pitch, pace, pause, and stress features.

Signal versions:
- v1: long positive Q&A tone after positive earnings surprise
- v2: short negative Q&A tone despite positive prepared remarks
- v3: avoid longs when transcript tone and voice affect diverge negatively
- v4: combine tone gap with analyst revision confirmation

Backtest rules:
- enter after call transcript/audio availability timestamp
- use conservative processing delay
- hold 5, 20, and 40 trading days
- test transcript-only before adding audio

Expected failure tests:
- CEO-only vs CFO-only features
- prepared remarks excluded vs included
- calls with missing speaker labels excluded
- sector-neutral and earnings-surprise-neutral variants

First Claude Code CLI task:
"Using BATCH 03, implement transcript preprocessing and feature specs for
prepared remarks, Q&A, speaker role, sentiment, uncertainty, and evasiveness.

BATCH 04: Product-Market Network and Rival-Shock Engine

Papers used:
- PAPER 07: Hoberg and Phillips
- PAPER 08: Hoberg, Phillips, and Prabhala

Strategy hypothesis:
Dynamic product-market peer networks reveal rival shocks, competitive threats,
and product similarity exposures missed by GICS sectors. S&P 500 firms may react
with delays to peer earnings, guidance, and product-market movement.

Data needed:
- 10-K product descriptions or Hoberg-Phillips TNIC/product similarity data
- product-market fluidity data if available
- earnings and guidance data for peers
- sector and industry classifications
- price and accounting data

Feature build:

BATCH 06: Innovation, Patent Value, and R&D Revaluation Engine

Papers used:
- PAPER 10: Hirshleifer, Hsu, and Li
- PAPER 11: Kogan, Papanikolaou, Seru, and Stoffman
- PAPER 12: Eberhart, Maxwell, and Siddique

Strategy hypothesis:
The market underreacts to productive innovation, especially when patent quality,
innovation efficiency, and unexpected R&D increases identify real future growth
rather than hype.

Data needed:
- Compustat R&D expense
- patent grants and citations
- patent value estimates if available
- sector classifications
- accounting fundamentals
- price and returns

Feature build:
1. Build R&D capital and R&D surprise.
2. Build patent count and citation-weighted patent count.
3. Build innovation efficiency: patent output per R&D input.
4. Build patent-value shock.
5. Confirm with operating performance follow-through.

Signal versions:
- v1: long high innovation efficiency firms
- v2: long unexpected R&D increases with balance-sheet strength
- v3: long patent-value shock, sector-neutral
- v4: avoid expensive innovation firms without patent/R&D follow-through

Backtest rules:
- hold 6, 12, and 24 months
- rebalance quarterly
- sector-neutral ranks
- test with and without mega-cap tech

Expected failure tests:
- exclude technology sector
- citation lag adjustment
- patent grant date vs application date
- R&D increases during distress excluded

- PAPER 15: Avramov, Chordia, Jostova, and Philipov

Strategy hypothesis:
Credit risk, rating state, and distance-to-default changes improve equity

- credit ratings and outlooks

Backtest rules:

- crisis-regime stress tests

BATCH 08: ETF Ownership, Passive Crowding, and Basket-Flow Fragility Engine

Papers used:
- PAPER 17: Ben-David, Franzoni, and Moussawi
- PAPER 18: Israeli, Lee, and Sridharan

Strategy hypothesis:
ETF ownership and ETF flow exposure change the volatility, synchronicity,
liquidity, and information environment of S&P 500 stocks. These features can
improve risk sizing and identify delayed firm-specific reactions after basket
pressure.

Data needed:
- ETF holdings by underlying stock
- ETF flows or shares outstanding
- S&P 500 stock returns and volumes
- intraday volatility if available
- spreads/liquidity proxies
- analyst coverage if available

Feature build:
1. Build ETF ownership percentage.
2. Build ETF ownership change.
3. Build ETF flow exposure by stock.
4. Build return synchronicity.
5. Build firm-specific residual return after market/sector/ETF moves.

Signal versions:
- v1: volatility forecast using ETF ownership and flow exposure
- v2: mean reversion after ETF-flow-driven residual dislocation
- v3: downweight firm-specific fundamental signals when synchronicity is high
- v4: long delayed firm-specific positive news after ETF-driven selloff

Backtest rules:
- daily or weekly rebalance
- use realistic holding data lag
- compare SPY/IVV/VOO-only exposure vs all ETF exposure
- separate signal generation from risk-sizing overlay

Expected failure tests:
- mega-cap subset
- high ETF ownership but no flow shock
- high beta stocks vs high ETF exposure stocks
- liquidity regimes

BATCH 09: Institutional Horizon and 13F Flow Engine

Papers used:
- PAPER 19: Bushee
- PAPER 20: Yan and Zhang

Strategy hypothesis:
Institutional ownership is more informative when separated by investor horizon.
Short-term institutional accumulation can predict returns and earnings surprises,
while transient ownership can also pressure management toward short-term
decisions.

Data needed:
- 13F holdings
- institution classification or inferred turnover
- S&P 500 constituents
- earnings data
- fundamentals, especially R&D and payout behavior
- price and returns

Feature build:
1. Infer institution turnover/horizon.
2. Classify owners as short-term, dedicated, or quasi-indexer.
3. Build ownership-flow features by horizon.
4. Build institutional accumulation before earnings.
5. Combine owner-horizon mix with R&D and earnings-pressure features.

Signal versions:
- v1: long short-term institutional accumulation
- v2: long accumulation before positive expected earnings surprise
- v3: downweight R&D signals when transient ownership is high and R&D cuts occur
- v4: long firms with dedicated ownership supporting high innovation efficiency

Backtest rules:
- use 13F filing publication date, not quarter-end holdings date
- quarterly rebalance
- test with realistic reporting lag
- compare active institutions vs index-like institutions

Expected failure tests:
- delayed publication destroys alpha
- top holders only vs full 13F universe
- ownership change due to index reconstitution
- sector and size neutrality

"Using BATCH 09, create a 13F ownership feature contract. Include publication
date vs holding date, institution horizon inference, short-term accumulation,
dedicated ownership, and tests for reporting-lag lookahead."
